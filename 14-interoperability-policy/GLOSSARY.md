# Glossary: 14 Interoperability policy

A few terms this course reuses are defined in the [curriculum-wide glossary](../start-here/GLOSSARY.md): HIPAA, FHIR, USCDI, ONC. The interoperability-policy vocabulary specific to this course appears below.

**Information blocking.** A practice by a health IT developer, health information network, health information exchange, or healthcare provider that, except as required by law or covered by an exception, is likely to interfere with the access, exchange, or use of electronic health information. The definition is statutory (21st Century Cures Act) and operational (ONC Cures Act Final Rule). The practice is the load-bearing concept of this course: the rest of the policy stack exists to prohibit it.

**21st Century Cures Act.** A 2016 federal law containing the statutory definition of information blocking, the Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement (TEFCA), and the requirements that healthcare providers, developers, and information networks share electronic health information. The information-blocking provisions are at Section 4004 of the law. Most of the technical mandates that followed (the ONC Cures Act Final Rule, the CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule) are implementations of this statute.

**HITECH Act.** The Health Information Technology for Economic and Clinical Health Act of 2009, the legislation that funded the Meaningful Use incentive program and produced the rapid EHR-adoption curve of the 2010s. HITECH is the precondition to this course: it put EHRs in nearly every clinical setting and thereby created the systems whose data the Cures Act later required to flow.

**Meaningful Use (Promoting Interoperability).** The Medicare and Medicaid EHR Incentive Program authorized by HITECH. Providers received incentive payments for adopting certified EHRs and meeting use criteria across three stages (2011 to 2018). The program was renamed Promoting Interoperability in 2018 and folded into the Merit-based Incentive Payment System (MIPS) for clinicians. Meaningful Use created the standards-conformant EHR base on which later interoperability policy was built.

**ONC (Office of the National Coordinator for Health Information Technology).** The HHS office that sets standards for certified health IT, runs the ONC Health IT Certification Program, and publishes the rules implementing the Cures Act information-blocking provisions and USCDI. ONC is the agency a vendor's certified EHR is certified by.

**CMS (Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services).** The HHS agency that pays for Medicare and Medicaid and that conditions those payments on data-exchange requirements. CMS's interoperability rules (the 2020 Patient Access Final Rule, the 2024 Advancing Interoperability and Improving Prior Authorization Final Rule) are the carrier-side complement to ONC's provider-and-developer-side rules.

**ONC Health IT Certification Program.** The program by which ONC certifies health IT products as conforming to specified standards. Certified EHRs (CEHRT) are required for Medicare and Medicaid reimbursement under Meaningful Use and the Quality Payment Program. Certification is the lever ONC uses to mandate FHIR, USCDI, and the information-blocking conditions.

**Cures Act Final Rule.** ONC's 2020 implementing regulation for the Cures Act information-blocking provisions and the Conditions and Maintenance of Certification for certified health IT. The rule names the eight exceptions to information blocking, requires standardized FHIR APIs for patient access, and mandates USCDI as the baseline data set.

**HTI-1 (Health Data, Technology, and Interoperability) Final Rule.** ONC's 2024 update to the Cures Act Final Rule. HTI-1 advances USCDI to v3, adds transparency requirements for Decision Support Interventions (DSI) embedded in certified health IT (including AI/ML models), updates the information-blocking definitions, and extends the FHIR API requirements. Effective dates roll out from 2024 through 2026.

**HTI-2 Final Rule.** ONC's 2024 proposed rule (finalized in stages 2024 to 2025) extending HTI-1. HTI-2 covers public health data exchange, the Trusted Exchange Framework requirements for certified health IT, and additional USCDI advancement. It is the second of an ongoing series of rules from ONC implementing the Cures Act mandate.

**USCDI (United States Core Data for Interoperability).** A standardized set of health data classes and elements that certified health IT must be capable of exchanging. USCDI evolves on roughly an annual cadence (v1 in 2020 through v6 in July 2025, with v7 in draft as of early 2026), each version adding data classes such as clinical tests, immunizations, social determinants, encounter detail, and so on. USCDI is the "what" that the FHIR APIs from Course 06 carry.

**CMS Interoperability and Patient Access Final Rule.** CMS's 2020 rule requiring CMS-regulated payers (Medicare Advantage, Medicaid managed care, ACA exchange plans) to implement a Patient Access API, a Provider Directory API, and Payer-to-Payer data exchange, all using FHIR R4 and USCDI. The rule is what gives patients programmatic access to their claims data, not only their clinical records.

**CMS Advancing Interoperability and Improving Prior Authorization Final Rule.** CMS's 2024 rule extending the Patient Access framework: requires payers to operate a Prior Authorization API, a Provider Access API, and a Payer-to-Payer API on FHIR-based standards, with the goal of automating prior-authorization workflows. Effective dates start in 2026 and 2027.

**Information-blocking exceptions.** The categories of practice that, while they may restrict information access, do not meet the definition of information blocking. The Cures Act Final Rule (2020) defined eight exceptions, grouped as practices that prevent harm, protect privacy, protect security, are infeasible, address health IT performance, and address content and manner, fees, and licensing of interoperability elements. HTI-1 (2024) and subsequent rulemaking added further exceptions (a TEFCA Manner Exception and Protecting Care Access), bringing the current count to ten. The exceptions are the operational distinction between blocking and legitimate restriction; the regulatory text lives at 45 CFR Part 171.

**TEFCA (Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement).** A federally-supported network of networks for nationwide health information exchange. TEFCA designates Qualified Health Information Networks (QHINs) that interconnect under the Common Agreement; participants in any QHIN can exchange data with participants in any other. TEFCA became operational in 2023 with the first QHINs designated, and continues to expand.

**QHIN (Qualified Health Information Network).** A network designated under TEFCA to interconnect with other QHINs and exchange data nationwide under the Common Agreement. As of 2024 several QHINs operate (eHealth Exchange, Epic Nexus, Health Gorilla, Kno2, MedAllies, KONZA, CommonWell), with more in the designation pipeline.

**Common Agreement.** The legal and technical agreement signed by QHINs to participate in TEFCA. The Common Agreement specifies the permitted purposes for exchange (treatment, payment, healthcare operations, public health, government benefits, individual access), the FHIR-based technical implementation, and the dispute-resolution mechanism.

**Right of access (HIPAA Privacy Rule, 45 CFR 164.524).** The HIPAA provision that gives an individual the right to inspect and obtain a copy of their protected health information in the designated record set, in the form and format requested when readily producible, within 30 days. The right of access predates the Cures Act and remains the foundational mechanism for patient access; the Cures Act extends it by also prohibiting practices that interfere with electronic access.

**OIG (Office of Inspector General, HHS).** The HHS office that enforces the information-blocking provisions against health IT developers and HIEs (through civil monetary penalties of up to $1 million per violation under a 2023 OIG final rule) and refers provider cases to CMS for disincentives. OIG is the enforcement endpoint when an information-blocking complaint is substantiated.

**GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation).** The EU's comprehensive data-protection law, in force since May 2018. GDPR applies to processing of personal data of EU residents regardless of where the processor is located. It establishes a lawful-basis framework (consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, legitimate interests), data-subject rights (access, rectification, erasure, portability, objection), and special protections for health data. GDPR sets a higher floor than HIPAA in several places: broader scope, stronger consent requirements, broader subject rights, larger penalties.

**Lawful basis (GDPR).** One of six grounds on which personal data may be processed: consent, performance of a contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task, or legitimate interests. For special category data (which includes health data), one of these six must be present and additionally an Article 9 condition (explicit consent, public-health task, scientific research with safeguards) must be met. The lawful-basis question has no direct U.S. analog; HIPAA permits use and disclosure for treatment, payment, and operations without consent.

**Special category data (GDPR).** Categories of personal data given heightened protection under Article 9 of GDPR: racial or ethnic origin, political opinions, religious beliefs, trade-union membership, genetic data, biometric data for identification, **health data**, and data concerning sex life or sexual orientation. Processing requires both an Article 6 lawful basis and an Article 9 condition.

**NHS Digital / NHS England.** The technology and data arm of the National Health Service in England. NHS Digital was merged into NHS England in 2023. It runs the Spine (the national infrastructure for patient data exchange), maintains the NHS Number, sets clinical standards (UK SNOMED CT is the primary terminology), and operates national digital services including the NHS App.

**NHS Number.** A unique 10-digit identifier assigned to every patient in England and Wales (and similar national IDs in Scotland and Northern Ireland). The NHS Number is the patient-matching substrate of the UK system: it is assigned at birth or first contact, is used universally across NHS services, and largely eliminates the patient-matching problem that persists in the U.S. without a national patient identifier.

**EHDS (European Health Data Space).** A 2025 EU regulation creating a common framework for both primary use (clinical care, patient access to records across borders) and secondary use (research, policy, regulatory) of health data across EU member states. EHDS mandates the European Electronic Health Record Exchange Format (EEHRxF), establishes Health Data Access Bodies in each member state for secondary use, and is the EU analog to the policy framework Tracks 02 and 03 cover.

**Primary use vs secondary use (EHDS).** A distinction EHDS makes explicit: primary use is healthcare delivery and patient access; secondary use is research, public-health surveillance, policy-making, and regulatory activities. EHDS regulates the two separately and gives patients distinct rights in each. The distinction echoes the Course 03 secondary-use question and the Course 13 sharing-tier framework.

**Patient matching.** The problem of identifying when records from different sources refer to the same person. The U.S. has no national patient identifier (a 1996 HIPAA provision authorizing one was defunded in 1998 and has remained so), so matching relies on combinations of demographics (name, date of birth, address, phone, SSN fragments). Match rates between health systems range from 50% to 90% depending on the algorithm and data quality; the unmatched fraction is one of the load-bearing remaining barriers to interoperability.

**Decision Support Interventions (DSI) (HTI-1).** The HTI-1 term for any computable algorithm, rule, or model integrated into a certified EHR that influences clinical decisions. DSI includes both rule-based clinical decision support (Course 12) and AI/ML predictive models (Course 09). HTI-1 requires certified developers to disclose specified source attributes for each DSI (intervention name, purpose, data inputs, intended use, output type, when it was tested, on whom it was tested, performance characteristics including subgroup performance).

**Civil monetary penalty (CMP, information blocking).** The financial penalty OIG can impose on an information-blocking health IT developer or HIE: up to $1,000,000 per violation under the 2023 final rule. The provider parallel is "disincentives" administered by CMS through the Medicare quality programs. The penalty structure is what gives the information-blocking prohibition operational force.

**PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act).** Canada's federal private-sector privacy law. PIPEDA covers commercial activity across provinces; several provinces (Alberta, British Columbia, Quebec) have substantially similar laws that apply locally. For health data specifically, each province has its own health-information privacy statute (PHIPA in Ontario, HIA in Alberta), which are the operational analogs of HIPAA at the provincial level.
