# Track 01: Why policy exists

A clinician asks for a patient's labs from another health system. The records arrive only by fax, three weeks later, in PDF, unreadable in the EHR's external-document tab. None of this is a technical limitation; it is a set of choices that any of several parties made. Federal policy gave that set of choices a name in 2016: information blocking. Track 01 covers what the term names, the precondition that produced it (a decade of HITECH and Meaningful Use that put EHRs in nearly every clinical setting, and that gave the resulting data economic value), and the analysis of who was benefiting from the status quo and how. Together those answer why the policy response took the form it did.

Three properties of information blocking are load-bearing for the rest of the course. The practice is defined in statute (21st Century Cures Act Section 4004), which is what gives federal agencies the authority to act on it. It is identifiable by effect rather than by intent (a practice that, except as required by law or covered by an exception, is *likely to interfere* with access, exchange, or use), which is what makes the prohibition enforceable without proof of motive. And since the 2023 OIG final rule, it has been backed by civil monetary penalties of up to $1,000,000 per violation against developers and HIEs, which is what converts the prohibition from a goal into a constraint.
