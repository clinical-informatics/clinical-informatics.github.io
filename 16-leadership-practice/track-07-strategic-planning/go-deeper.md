## Go deeper: Strategic planning and IT portfolio management

**If you want to read a complete, real strategic plan end to end:**

- [Global strategy on digital health 2020-2025 (World Health Organization)](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240020924) is a published, free, 50-page strategic plan for digital health, and the value here is structural rather than topical. Read it the way an IT Steering member reads a plan: find the vision statement, trace each strategic objective back to it, and notice how the action framework sequences commitments rather than listing wishes. The same anatomy (mission link, objectives, sequenced actions, governance for the refresh cycle) is what a health system's 3-year IT roadmap has at one-hundredth the scale.

**If you want to see how real institutions govern and prioritize the request queue:**

- [Governance of Electronic Health Record Modification at U.S. Academic Medical Centers (Ravi et al., Applied Clinical Informatics, 2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10599807/) reports interviews with the CMIOs of 18 academic medical centers about how EHR change requests are scored and decided. The findings ground this track's claims in practice: roughly a third of sites use quantitative scoring, a third qualitative, and a third none; request volume is the most-reported governance challenge; and few sites had defined metrics for whether their governance works. The variability is the lesson: the portfolio discipline this track presents is far from universal, and an informaticist who brings explicit scoring criteria to the table supplies what a third of these sites lack entirely.

**If you want evidence that governance changes what IT investment buys:**

- [Valuing Hospital Investment in Information Technology: Does Governance Make a Difference? (Parente and Van Horn, Health Care Financing Review, 2006)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4194982/) analyzes IT investment across roughly 2,800 U.S. hospitals and finds that the same category of spending produced different outcomes under different governance: for-profit hospitals converted IT into reduced inpatient bed days, not-for-profits into increased service volume. Each institution's IT portfolio served its own objective function. The result is the empirical form of this track's central claim: an IT investment has no value in the abstract, only value relative to the mission it traces to.
