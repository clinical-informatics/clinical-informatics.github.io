## What "data quality" actually means

The phrase "data quality" gets used loosely. Sometimes it means "the values are accurate." Sometimes it means "the records are complete." Sometimes it means "the dataset is fit for purpose." The most-cited working framework in clinical informatics is from Weiskopf and Weng (2013), and it gives the phrase five separable meanings.

Each meaning is a *dimension*. A dataset can be excellent on one and terrible on another. Saying "the data is bad" is usually shorthand for "the data is failing on dimension X for use case Y." Naming the dimension and the use case is most of what makes a data-quality conversation productive.

### The five dimensions

- **Completeness.** Are the values present when they should be? A flowsheet row for DAS28 should exist for every rheumatology visit at which DAS28 was computed. If half of them are missing, the completeness of DAS28 capture is 50% for that population.
- **Correctness.** Does the recorded value match what actually happened or was true? A potassium value of 12 mmol/L was almost certainly not the patient's potassium; somebody typed wrong, or the specimen was hemolyzed and the system did not flag it. Correctness is about the relationship between the chart and reality.
- **Concordance.** Do different parts of the record agree with each other? Ms. Reyes's allergy of "penicillin" may appear on the problem list, on the allergy list, and not at all in the medication-administration record, depending on the system that captured it.
- **Plausibility.** Is the value within clinically reasonable bounds? CRP of 5,000 mg/L is implausible (the assay does not go that high). Heart rate of 800 is implausible (the heart cannot beat that fast). Plausibility is the cheap range-check version of correctness, useful because it catches a lot of the typo-class failures without needing access to ground truth.
- **Currency.** Is the value up to date for the question being asked? A medication marked "active" that was discontinued two years ago fails currency. A problem-list entry of "obesity, BMI 32" is failing currency if the patient lost weight.

Two more dimensions show up in the wider data-quality literature and are worth knowing even though Weiskopf and Weng folded them into the five above:

- **Conformance.** Does the data follow the standard structure and coding? A LOINC-coded result follows the LOINC system; a local-code-only result fails conformance to the agreed terminology.
- **Uniqueness.** Is each entity (patient, encounter, order) represented exactly once? Duplicate patients fail uniqueness.

The five-dimension framing is enough for most informatics work. The two additional concepts (conformance, uniqueness) are useful when the conversation needs more granularity.

## The recurring problem families

Across thousands of clinical data extracts, the same families of issue come up. Here is the working set every informaticist should be able to spot.

### 1. Duplicate patients (uniqueness, concordance)

The same human being is represented under two or more distinct patient identifiers. The two MRNs may have slightly different demographics (typo in the spelling, swapped first and last name, two-digit difference in the DOB), or they may have identical demographics but the registration system created a new MRN by accident.

The structural fix is **patient matching**. Probabilistic matching algorithms compute a similarity score across multiple attributes (name, DOB, address, phone, SSN) and produce a merge candidate. The Fellegi-Sunter framework (1969) is the foundational mathematical model; modern Enterprise Master Patient Index (EMPI) products are sophisticated implementations of it. Open-source approaches like OHDSI's tools handle the simpler cases.

Even after matching, *unresolved* candidate pairs are common: pairs that look like the same person but are not, or pairs that look like different people but are. Manual review is the residual workload.

### 2. Inconsistent coding (concordance, conformance)

The same clinical concept is recorded with different codes across systems, or even within the same system across time. Penicillin allergy may be ICD-10 `Z88.0`, RxNorm `7980`, SNOMED CT `91936005`, the institution's local allergy code `ALG-PCN`, or the free-text string `PCN allergy`. A query that looks for one of these codes finds a subset of the patients with the allergy and misses the rest.

The fix is a **value set**: a curated list of codes that all mean the same concept, used as the query filter. Value sets are maintained at the institutional or national level (the National Library of Medicine's Value Set Authority Center, VSAC, is the US-standard registry). CDS Hooks and CQL (course 12) both depend on value sets to work.

### 3. Missing structured data (completeness)

A fact exists in the chart but only in the narrative; the structured slot is empty. Most common examples: vital signs from outside encounters, scores like DAS28 or PHQ-9 when the clinician computed them but did not click into the flowsheet, anti-coagulation goals, smoking status changes between encounters.

The fix is structured-capture promotion (workflow changes), supplemented by NLP for the cases where workflow change is not feasible. Course 10 of this curriculum is the NLP side. Course 17 (workflow, safety, human factors) is the workflow side.

### 4. Note-only findings (completeness)

Different from missing structured data in a subtle but important way: there is no structured slot to put the finding in, regardless of clinician motivation. Sharp/van der Heijde scoring (Track 04) is the canonical example. Radiology PDF readings are full of these.

The fix is specialty-specific structured templates plus, again, NLP. The trend is slow but real.

### 5. Terminology drift (conformance)

Code systems migrate over time. ICD-9 became ICD-10 in 2015 (later in some places). LOINC versions update yearly. Local codes get added when new tests are introduced and not retired when old tests are discontinued. The result is that historical extracts use a mix of code-system versions, and the mappings between them are not always complete.

The fix is **vocabulary management**: somebody owns the mapping tables, reviews them periodically, and makes sure that conceptually-equivalent codes from different versions are linked. OMOP's vocabulary tables (course 07) are the most-mature open implementation of this; institutional vocabularies are usually maintained by a dedicated terminology services team.

### 6. Plausibility failures (plausibility, correctness)

Out-of-range values that almost certainly are not real. CRP of 5,000 mg/L. Heart rate of 800. Blood pressure of 30 over 18. Temperature in Fahrenheit recorded as if it were Celsius.

The fix is value-level range checks at extract time, with LOINC-aware reference ranges. The thresholds should be *clinically* implausible, not statistically extreme, so that real outliers (sepsis with a heart rate of 180) are not dropped. The Achilles framework from OHDSI is a widely-used tool for running these checks at scale.

### 7. Currency failures

Medications still flagged active that have been discontinued. Problem list entries for resolved conditions. Allergy records that the patient has since shown they do not actually have. Address records from five moves ago.

The fix is medication and problem-list reconciliation at every visit, paired with periodic record-cleanup campaigns at the institutional level. Meaningful Use rewarded structured reconciliation; the cleanup work is ongoing.

### 8. MRN drift after merges

When two MRNs for the same patient are merged, historical records under the *old* MRN may or may not be updated to the new MRN. The data warehouse should propagate the merge, but propagations sometimes lag, and historical extracts that pre-date the merge always show the old MRN.

The fix is to always pull through a **patient-identifier crosswalk** that maps every historical MRN to its current canonical MRN. The crosswalk lives in the EMPI; queries should join through it rather than filtering by current MRN directly.

### 9. Phantom encounters

Encounters that exist in the encounter table but never actually happened. Causes: a registration was started and then canceled (the registration row was created but not deleted), or a no-show was not marked as such, or a test patient was created for training and never cleaned up.

The fix is to filter on encounter status, completion flag, and (defensively) the presence of any associated clinical activity. An encounter with zero notes, zero results, zero orders, and zero charges is probably not a real encounter.

## What this looks like in practice

A real institutional data-quality program has the following layers, roughly:

1. **Extract-time validation.** Every nightly ETL run computes a set of summary statistics (row counts, distinct patient counts, expected value-range proportions) and alerts if any of them drift outside the historical bounds.
2. **Periodic data audits.** A team reviews samples of recent extracts against the chart, looking for the problem families above. The findings drive workflow changes upstream.
3. **A vocabulary services team.** Someone owns the mapping tables, the local codes, the value sets, and the standard-to-local crosswalks.
4. **An EMPI and patient-matching practice.** Patient identity reconciliation runs continuously; manual review of unresolved pairs is part of someone's job.
5. **An OMOP or other CDM mapping pass.** For research-grade analytical work, the institution maps to a standardized CDM. The mapping exposes problems that the institutional CDW had been quietly hiding.
6. **Data provenance documentation** (course 13). Every transformation gets logged. Every fix is reproducible.

This is unglamorous work. It is also the work that makes the difference between an analytical result a clinical leader will act on and one they will (correctly) dismiss.

## The reflex to install

When a data extract lands on your desk, the first pass is the classifier pass: walk the seven-or-so recurring problem families against what is in front of you, name each finding against a Weiskopf-Weng dimension, decide which findings the analytical use case forces you to fix, and document what you did so the next analyst can decide whether to trust the result. The dimension determines the fix. The use case determines which fixes are worth the cost. The documentation is what makes the result defensible. Course 13 picks up the documentation discipline; the course capstone here exercises the rest.
