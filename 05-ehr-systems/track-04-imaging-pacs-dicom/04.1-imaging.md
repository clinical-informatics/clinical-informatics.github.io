# 04.1 Imaging informatics

A reference essay for Track 04. Read this for the architectural depth and the historical context.

## Why imaging is its own subsystem

In every other domain we have covered, the operational EHR is the system of record. Medications, problems, allergies, labs, encounters, vitals: the EHR holds them and the chart renders them. Imaging is the place where the EHR is *not* the system of record.

The reason is a combination of three constraints:

1. **The data is huge.** A 16-bit, 2,500-by-2,048 digital radiograph is 10 megabytes per image. A typical CT chest is 300 to 1,500 slices at 0.5 MB each, so 150 MB to 750 MB per study. A high-resolution mammography study can exceed a gigabyte. The operational EHR database has no business holding any of that.
2. **The display is specialized.** Radiologists read on calibrated medical-grade monitors at known luminance, with pixel-level tools the EHR cannot reasonably embed. The reading-room viewer is a real piece of software (it is sometimes a full-fledged workstation product). The EHR-side viewer is the lightweight alternative for non-radiologist clinicians.
3. **The workflow predates the EHR.** Hospital radiology departments were running RIS-and-PACS configurations in the 1990s, before most hospitals had an EHR. The DICOM standard was published in 1993 specifically to standardize image exchange. By the time the modern EHR was built, the imaging subsystem already had its own protocols, its own vendors, and its own conventions.

So imaging lives in its own four-system architecture: EHR for ordering, RIS for workflow, modality for acquisition, PACS for storage. The EHR-embedded viewer is a window into the PACS, not a copy of the PACS. The radiology report is the artifact that does cross over to the EHR proper.

## DICOM in some depth

### The file format

A DICOM file is a stream of bytes organized as a sequence of *data elements*. Each data element is:

- A **tag** (`group, element`), four bytes total, identifying the attribute.
- A **value representation (VR)**, two bytes, giving the data type (PN for person name, DA for date, IS for integer string, OB for other byte, ...).
- A **length** giving the byte length of the value.
- The **value** itself.

The data elements are ordered by tag, in ascending order. The pixel data (tag `(7FE0, 0010)`) is the last element in the file, since it dominates the file size.

DICOM files almost always have a 128-byte preamble followed by a four-byte `DICM` magic header, so a tool can verify it is reading a DICOM file. Vendors sometimes embed a TIFF or JPEG fallback in the preamble for compatibility with non-DICOM image viewers.

### The information model

DICOM organizes clinical imaging into a four-level hierarchy:

1. **Patient.** Identified by the `(0010, 0020)` Patient ID tag. Usually equal to the MRN, sometimes the result of a patient-merge operation.
2. **Study.** Identified by Study Instance UID `(0020, 000D)`. One study is one ordered imaging session for one patient. A bilateral hand series is a study.
3. **Series.** Identified by Series Instance UID `(0020, 000E)`. One series is a coherent set of instances acquired together. For a hand radiograph, each view (PA right, PA left, oblique) is its own series. For a CT, a single contrast phase is one series, and a study often contains multiple series.
4. **Instance.** Identified by SOP Instance UID `(0008, 0018)`. One instance is one image (or one slice, for cross-sectional modalities).

Every tag at the patient level is supposed to be identical across all studies for the same patient. Every tag at the study level is supposed to be identical across all series in that study. The hierarchy is enforced loosely: the standard expects consistency but does not strictly require it, and a real-world PACS often has to reconcile minor disagreements between same-study series produced by the same modality.

### The SOP class

A DICOM instance carries a **SOP Class UID** `(0008, 0016)` that identifies what kind of object it is. Common SOP classes:

- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.1.1` Digital X-Ray Image (for presentation)
- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.2` CT Image
- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.4` MR Image
- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.6.1` Ultrasound Image
- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.88.11` Basic Text Structured Report
- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.88.33` Comprehensive Structured Report
- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.88.67` X-Ray Radiation Dose Structured Report
- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.7` Secondary Capture Image (for screen captures and exports)
- `1.2.840.10008.5.1.4.1.1.104.1` Encapsulated PDF (for PDF reports stored as DICOM)

The SOP Class tells a receiver what to expect. A PACS storing a CT image and a PACS storing a structured report use the same DICOM protocol but very different downstream handling.

### The protocol family

DICOM has two protocol families:

- **DIMSE** (DICOM Message Service Element). The legacy protocol. Verb-based: C-STORE to push an instance, C-FIND to query, C-MOVE to retrieve, C-GET to retrieve a smaller set. Runs over a custom TCP protocol on port 104 (or whichever port the vendors configured). Predates HTTP. Built around the assumption that the imaging device and the PACS are on the same LAN. Still ubiquitous between modality and PACS.
- **DICOMweb**. The modern alternative built on HTTPS. Three services:
  - **QIDO-RS** (Query). Search for studies, series, or instances using REST-style URLs and JSON responses.
  - **WADO-RS** (Retrieve). Retrieve studies, series, or instances by URL, with content negotiation for JSON metadata, original DICOM, JPEG renderings, or other representations.
  - **STOW-RS** (Store). Submit new DICOM objects via HTTP POST.

DICOMweb is the standard the field has been adopting since the early 2010s. As of 2026, most new PACS products support it natively, and most web-based viewers (EHR-embedded viewers, SMART-on-FHIR imaging apps, AI tools) speak it. The transition is partial. Legacy modalities still speak DIMSE; legacy PACS deployments sometimes only expose DIMSE outward. The interface layer (often a hospital's enterprise imaging vendor-neutral archive, or VNA) handles the protocol translation when needed.

## RIS and the workflow plumbing

The RIS is the imaging-equivalent of the operational EHR for the radiology department. It manages:

- **Order management.** Incoming orders from the EHR. ICD-10 reasons. Procedure codes. CPT and HCPCS. Priority. Indication free text.
- **Scheduling.** Allocating modality time and patient slots.
- **Modality Worklist.** A DICOM service the RIS provides to each modality. When a patient arrives, the radiographer queries the worklist on the imaging device, picks the matching order, and the device pre-populates Patient Name, Patient ID, Accession Number, and Procedure into the DICOM header automatically. The Modality Worklist is the single best thing in clinical imaging informatics; before it existed, radiographers typed patient identifiers into modalities by hand, and the error rate was awful.
- **Reporting.** Receive the radiologist's dictation, run it through speech-to-text or hold it as audio, build the final report, attach the structured report if applicable, send the result back to the EHR.

Vendor examples: Epic Radiant (Epic's integrated RIS), Oracle Cerner RadNet, Philips IntelliSpace Radiology, GE Healthcare Centricity RIS, Sectra RIS.

In a fully Epic shop, the RIS and the EHR are the same product, packaged as different modules of it. In a hospital that uses Epic for the EHR and a different vendor for the RIS, the two communicate via HL7 v2 messages, and most of the interface work for an imaging-related project goes into those interfaces.

## PACS and the storage tiering question

A PACS is sized very differently from an EHR database. Two architectural facts:

- **Most of the data is cold.** The operational EHR's hot data is whatever is in this week's charts. The PACS's hot data is whatever the radiologists are reading right now, plus comparison priors from the last twelve months. Studies older than a year or two account for the majority of stored data and a small minority of reads.
- **The data is immutable.** A DICOM instance, once stored, does not change. PACS storage is dominated by writes-once-read-occasionally objects, which is exactly the workload object storage is optimized for. Modern PACS architectures lean on object storage (Amazon S3, Azure Blob, on-prem MinIO) for the bulk of the corpus, with a flash cache for hot studies.

Track 01 covered the tiering question generically. The PACS-specific version is:

| Tier | Cutoff | Recall latency target | Where it sits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hot | Current to two years | Under one second | Flash, often local to the PACS server |
| Warm | Two to five years | A few seconds | SSD or enterprise SAN |
| Cool | Five to ten years | Tens of seconds | HDD-backed object storage |
| Cold | Ten years and older | Minutes to hours | Cold-archive object storage (Glacier-class) or tape |

The cold-tier recall latency is the place a hospital has to be careful. If the patient walks into the rheumatology clinic with hand pain and the comparison study from twelve years ago lives on cold tier, the radiologist cannot do the comparison in the same visit. Most hospitals build their tiering policy around the *clinical* recall pattern (RA patients keep coming back, so their priors stay warm longer) rather than a uniform age-based rule.

### The vendor-neutral archive

Many enterprises have layered a **VNA** (vendor-neutral archive) above the PACS. The VNA is the institutional long-term store, often cloud-based, that holds everything from every department's PACS in a single repository. The VNA handles cross-vendor compatibility, lifecycle management, and the trans-departmental sharing of images (cardiology, dermatology, pathology, ophthalmology, dentistry all produce DICOM-or-similar imaging data; the VNA is where they meet).

## Structured reporting in real life

DICOM SR (Structured Reporting) is the cleanest piece of the imaging-informatics standards portfolio and the most underused.

The standard is good. A DICOM SR is a tree of templated nodes, each tied to a SNOMED CT or DICOM-defined code. A modified Sharp/van der Heijde scoring report is a perfectly natural fit: total score, erosion subscore, joint-space narrowing subscore, per-joint findings, comparison.

The clinical reality is that most reports are still narrative PDFs. The reasons are not technical:

1. **Radiologist workflow.** Dictation is faster than form-filling for general reads.
2. **Template proliferation and inconsistency.** No single specialty consensus on the templates a radiologist should be using.
3. **Receiver tolerance.** The EHR is happy with a PDF. There is no pressure on the radiologist to produce SR.

The places where SR has taken hold are the specialties that adopted standardized templates:

- **Mammography** with BI-RADS, codified by the ACR.
- **Lung cancer screening** with Lung-RADS.
- **Prostate MRI** with PI-RADS.
- **Liver imaging** with LI-RADS.

In each of those, the structured-template radiology report ships as DICOM SR because the specialty's reporting convention already encoded the structure. Outside the RADS family, general radiology reports remain mostly PDF.

The increasing pressure for SR comes from two places: AI tools (Course 09) that produce structured findings as DICOM SR objects, and registry reporting requirements that require structured fields. The pressure is real but slow.

## The operational consequence

When an image is not showing up on a chart, the troubleshooting trace runs through the four-system architecture: EHR (did the order leave?), RIS (did the order schedule?), modality (did the device produce the DICOM file?), PACS (did the file arrive and index correctly?). Any DICOM viewer (dcm4che, MicroDicom, Horos, OHIF) will let you open the file and walk the patient, study, series, and instance tags. Modern interfaces use DICOMweb; legacy ones use DIMSE; the vendor-neutral archive in the middle often speaks both. And when the radiologist's score is sitting in a PDF instead of a structured field, most of the analytical loss at the imaging-to-EHR boundary lives in that single fact.
