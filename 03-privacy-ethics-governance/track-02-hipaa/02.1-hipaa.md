## The legal floor

HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996) is the federal floor in the United States for the handling of clinical data. The relevant pieces for this curriculum are the Privacy Rule (2003) and the Security Rule (2005). Together they specify what entities may do with **Protected Health Information** (PHI), how they must protect it, and under what circumstances they may share it.

HIPAA sets minimum requirements. Institutions may impose stricter standards. States may legislate stricter standards. International partners may demand stricter standards. HIPAA compliance is necessary but rarely sufficient.

HIPAA is also a partial floor. It applies only to **covered entities** and their **business associates**. The Apple Watch reading your heart rate is not a covered entity. The wellness app you used during pregnancy is not a covered entity. The employer wellness program that collected your BMI is, in most states, not a covered entity. Each of these holds data that would be PHI inside a hospital and is not PHI inside its own system. The regulatory floor stops at the hospital walls.

## The vocabulary that runs everything

### Protected Health Information (PHI)

PHI is health information that is **individually identifiable** and held or transmitted by a **covered entity** or a **business associate**. Three pieces:

- **Individually identifiable** means the information either identifies the individual or there is a reasonable basis to believe it could be used to identify them. The eighteen Safe Harbor categories below are the operational answer to "what does individually identifiable mean?"
- **Health information** means anything created or received by a healthcare provider, health plan, employer, or healthcare clearinghouse that relates to physical or mental health, the provision of care, or payment.
- **Held by a covered entity or business associate** is the jurisdictional limit. Outside that scope, the same information is not legally PHI.

### Covered Entity (CE)

Three categories. **Healthcare providers** that conduct electronic transactions (almost all hospitals, clinics, physicians, dentists, pharmacies). **Health plans** (insurers, HMOs, Medicare, Medicaid, employer-sponsored plans). **Healthcare clearinghouses** (entities that process claims data between providers and payers).

### Business Associate (BA)

A person or entity that performs functions for a CE that involve the use or disclosure of PHI. Examples: a billing service, a cloud-based EHR vendor, a research analytics consultant, a transcription service. Business associates are bound to HIPAA through a **Business Associate Agreement (BAA)** with the covered entity. After the HITECH Act (2009), BAs are directly liable for many HIPAA violations.

### The Four Pillars

- **Privacy Rule.** What uses and disclosures of PHI are permitted, what requires authorization, what de-identification means.
- **Security Rule.** Administrative, physical, and technical safeguards required for electronic PHI (ePHI).
- **Breach Notification Rule.** What constitutes a breach and how it must be reported (to affected individuals, to HHS, and sometimes to the media).
- **Enforcement Rule.** How OCR (the Office for Civil Rights) investigates and penalizes violations.

## The two paths to de-identification

The Privacy Rule defines two methods by which PHI can be de-identified, after which the data is no longer PHI and can be shared more freely.

### Safe Harbor: the checklist method

Remove all of the following eighteen categories of identifiers, and the resulting dataset is considered de-identified under HIPAA:

1. **Names** (including initials, nicknames).
2. **Geographic subdivisions smaller than a state.** Including street address, city, county, precinct, ZIP code, and equivalent geocodes. **Exception:** 3-digit ZIPs are allowed if the population of all ZIPs sharing those three digits is over 20,000; for ZIPs under 20,000, the 3-digit prefix must be `000`.
3. **All elements of dates (except year)** that directly relate to an individual: birth date, admission date, discharge date, date of death. All ages over 89 must be aggregated to "90 or older."
4. **Telephone numbers.**
5. **Vehicle identifiers** and serial numbers, including license plate numbers.
6. **Fax numbers.**
7. **Device identifiers** and serial numbers.
8. **Email addresses.**
9. **Web URLs.**
10. **Social Security numbers.**
11. **IP addresses.**
12. **Medical record numbers.**
13. **Biometric identifiers**, including finger and voice prints.
14. **Health plan beneficiary numbers.**
15. **Full-face photographs** and any comparable images.
16. **Account numbers.**
17. **Any other unique identifying number, characteristic, or code.**
18. **Certificate or license numbers.**

Plus a residual catch-all: the covered entity must have **no actual knowledge** that the residual information could be used alone or in combination with other information to identify the individual.

Safe Harbor is mechanical, easy to verify, and the conservative default. It is also conservative on data utility. Full dates of service are removed, ages over 89 are aggregated, and ZIPs are coarsened. For many research questions (longitudinal trajectories, time-to-event analyses, geographic patterns), Safe Harbor strips out the variables that drive the analysis.

### Expert Determination: the actuarial method

The Privacy Rule's alternative: a person with **appropriate knowledge of and experience with generally accepted statistical and scientific principles and methods for rendering information not individually identifiable** certifies that, applying such principles, the risk is very small that the information could be used (alone or in combination with other reasonably available information) to identify an individual. The expert must document the methods and results of the analysis.

In practice, an Expert Determination engagement involves a privacy specialist (often a PhD statistician with a privacy background) who:

- Maps the dataset's quasi-identifiers (Track 01 vocabulary).
- Estimates re-identification risk under specific attack scenarios.
- Recommends a combination of generalization, suppression, and noise injection to bring risk below a documented threshold.
- Writes a determination memo describing the assumptions, the methods, and the threshold.

Expert Determination is more expensive than Safe Harbor and produces a more utility-preserving dataset. Large research data networks (TriNetX, several major academic CDWs) typically operate on Expert-Determined datasets.

## Limited Data Sets: the middle path

A **Limited Data Set (LDS)** is a dataset that retains *some* identifiers normally stripped by Safe Harbor (specifically: full dates of service, city, state, ZIP, and 89+ ages can be kept) but still has the obvious direct identifiers removed.

An LDS is still PHI under HIPAA. It can be shared for research, public health, or healthcare operations *only* under a **Data Use Agreement (DUA)** that:

- Names the specific purposes for which the data may be used.
- Names the people and entities permitted to receive and use the data.
- Prohibits re-identification attempts.
- Requires appropriate safeguards.
- Requires reporting any unauthorized uses or disclosures.
- Requires the recipient to ensure that any subcontractors also follow these terms.

An LDS with a DUA is the operational middle ground for much secondary-use clinical research. It preserves dates of service (needed for almost all longitudinal analysis), allows ZIP-level geographic analysis, and treats the patient population over 89 as a real population rather than an aggregated lump. It does require the contractual layer to be in place.

## When you need IRB review

IRB (Institutional Review Board) review is required for **human-subjects research**. The two operational regulatory frameworks are the **Common Rule** (45 CFR 46) and, for FDA-regulated research, **FDA's parallel regulations** (21 CFR 50 and 56). HIPAA interacts with both: an IRB can waive the requirement for individual HIPAA authorization for research under specific conditions (the data must be necessary; the research couldn't be done without it; the privacy risk is minimal).

The categories worth knowing:

- **Not human-subjects research.** Quality improvement projects, internal operational analyses, public health surveillance, education evaluations. Often does not require IRB review at all, though institutions sometimes require a determination letter from the IRB stating this.
- **Exempt research.** Research that uses *existing*, de-identified data (or coded data without access to the key), or that consists of certain types of educational or anonymous survey research. Lighter touch: typically a determination by the IRB that the research is exempt, often without full board review.
- **Expedited review.** Minimal-risk research that doesn't qualify for exemption. Reviewed by a single IRB member rather than the full board.
- **Full board review.** Greater-than-minimal-risk research. Reviewed by the full IRB at a convened meeting.

A common clinical-informatics scenario: research using de-identified EHR data is **often exempt** but not always. Exemption depends on whether the dataset is truly de-identified and on what activities the research includes. Get an exemption determination from your local IRB rather than assume it.

## Beyond HIPAA

HIPAA is the floor, not the ceiling. The relevant additional layers:

### State laws

- **California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA) / California Privacy Rights Act (CPRA).** Broader privacy framework that includes health data not covered by HIPAA (apps, wellness data). Provides individual rights to access, delete, and opt out of data sale.
- **Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA).** Specific protections for biometric identifiers (fingerprint, retina scan, voice print). Has produced significant litigation.
- **Washington My Health My Data Act (2023).** First state law specifically targeting "consumer health data" outside HIPAA's reach (apps, wellness trackers, mental-health apps, location data near healthcare facilities).
- **Texas, New York, Connecticut, Colorado, Virginia, and a growing list of others.** Each adds protections beyond the federal floor, sometimes overlapping, sometimes layered.

### 42 CFR Part 2

A separate, *stricter* federal framework for substance use disorder treatment records. Information about a patient's substance use disorder treatment cannot be disclosed without specific written consent for each disclosure, even to other healthcare providers. (HIPAA permits disclosures between providers for treatment without specific patient authorization; 42 CFR Part 2 does not.) The framework has been substantially aligned with HIPAA by 2024 rule changes but remains distinct.

### GDPR

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (2018) applies to any organization processing personal data of EU residents, regardless of where the organization is located. For clinical research with international participants or international data sharing, GDPR is the layer above HIPAA. The protections are different in kind: GDPR treats health data as a special category requiring explicit consent or specific legal bases, gives individuals broader rights (access, correction, erasure, portability), and provides for substantial fines.

### The apps/wearables gap

HIPAA does not cover health data collected directly by consumers from apps, wearables, direct-to-consumer genetic testing, or wellness programs run outside healthcare. Your Apple Watch heart rate is not PHI. Your 23andMe results are not PHI. Your menstrual cycle tracker's data is not PHI. The gap is real and politically contested. Post-Dobbs, several states have moved to fill it with consumer-health-data statutes (Washington's MHMDA, for example), but federal action has lagged.

For a clinical researcher, if a study collects data from a wearable that the patient owns, the device's TOS and the consent form (not HIPAA) typically govern. If the same data flows into the EHR through a regulated digital health intervention, it becomes PHI at that point.

## Putting it together: the decision tree

The interactive in the notebook is a pathway selector. The underlying logic:

1. **Is this PHI?** If no covered entity is involved and the data isn't being held by one, HIPAA may not apply.
2. **Is the research using identifiable PHI?** If yes, you need IRB approval (or a documented exemption) plus a consent or HIPAA authorization waiver.
3. **Can the research question be answered with de-identified data?** If yes, prefer de-identification. Choose the path:
   - Safe Harbor is appropriate when the research question doesn't need dates of service or geographic detail finer than 3-digit ZIP.
   - Expert Determination is appropriate when more granular data is needed and the resources support a privacy specialist.
   - Limited Data Set with a DUA is appropriate when dates of service or geographic detail are needed *and* the research is being conducted by parties who can enter a DUA.
4. **What state and international layers apply?** If subjects include California, Illinois, Washington, EU, or other regulated jurisdictions, those frameworks layer on top.

The decision is never "HIPAA is fine" without specifying which path; the four paths (Safe Harbor, Expert Determination, LDS with DUA, full PHI with IRB authorization) buy different things and cost different things.

## Scope of this track

This track is a working vocabulary for the conversation, not a substitute for your IRB and privacy office. It is not legal advice. Privacy law is jurisdiction-specific, fact-specific, and evolving. The framework here is the conceptual foundation. The operational decision needs the actual experts in the actual jurisdiction. The state laws and international frameworks above are the ones that come up most often in US clinical-informatics work as of the curriculum date. New state laws are passing routinely. Check the current landscape when you have a specific question.
