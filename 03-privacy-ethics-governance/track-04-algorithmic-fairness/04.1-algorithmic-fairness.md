## Algorithms are not neutral

A clinical decision rule, a risk score, a deployed AI tool: all of them are decisions encoded. The people who built them made choices. Whose data trained it. Which outcome to predict. Which features to include. Which threshold to recommend. Each choice shapes who benefits and who does not. When the choices are made carelessly, the resulting tool inherits the disparities of the system that produced it. When they are made deliberately, the tool can correct for some of those disparities. It cannot eliminate all of them.

This track is the working clinician's framework for evaluating any clinical algorithm. It is not a course on the mathematics of fairness. It is the vocabulary, the case studies, and the questions to ask before a model is allowed into your workflow.

## The five places bias enters

A clinical algorithm is the end of a pipeline. Bias can enter the pipeline at any of five points. Each one has been documented in the literature.

### 1. Training data: who is in the dataset

If the model is trained on data from one population and deployed in a different population, the model may not perform on the deployment population the way the validation numbers suggested. The most common version is academic medical centers. AMC patients tend to be more insured, more white, more in the catchment area of major cities, and more in research-engaged populations. A model trained on AMC data and deployed in a safety-net hospital with predominantly Medicaid and Medicare patients has crossed a population boundary the model did not know about.

Examples documented:

- **Dermatology AI models** trained primarily on light-skinned subjects perform worse on darker skin tones (Adamson and Smith, JAMA Dermatology, 2018).
- **Chest X-ray AI** systematically *underdiagnoses* under-represented populations: women, Black patients, Hispanic patients, low-income patients (Seyyed-Kalantari et al., Nature Medicine, 2021).
- **Sepsis prediction models** built at one institution generalize poorly to others.

### 2. Labels: what the outcome variable actually measures

The label is the outcome the model learns to predict. It is rarely the thing you actually care about. It is a *proxy* for what you care about. If the proxy is biased, the model that predicts the proxy is biased.

This is the most subtle source of bias and the most consequential. The Obermeyer 2019 case (next section) is the canonical example. The algorithm predicted *cost* as a proxy for *need*. Because Black patients receive less spending for any given level of illness, the algorithm systematically rated them as less needy than equivalently sick White patients.

Other clinical proxy problems:

- **"Length of stay" as a proxy for severity.** LOS is partly severity, partly insurance status, partly social factors. A model predicting LOS is not a model predicting severity.
- **"Readmission within 30 days" as a proxy for quality of discharge.** Readmissions reflect quality, but also access to follow-up care, social support, and post-discharge resources, which are not evenly distributed.
- **"ICU transfer" as a proxy for clinical deterioration.** ICU transfer is partly clinical, partly bed availability, partly the unit culture.

The honest version of every algorithm spec answers two questions: what is the label, and what construct is it standing in for? If the answer to the second question is unsatisfying, the model will not measure the construct correctly.

### 3. Features: proxies for protected characteristics

Even if "race" is not in the feature list, the model may infer it from features that correlate with race. ZIP code, insurance type, primary care provider, language spoken at home, medication list, even handwriting in scanned forms have all been shown to encode race indirectly.

Two opposite patterns to watch for:

- **Inadvertent encoding.** The modeler removed "race" but kept ZIP code. The model learned ZIP by ZIP that some ZIPs (with predominantly minority populations) have systematically worse outcomes, and now predicts worse outcomes for those ZIPs in a way that affects clinical decisions.
- **Explicit race correction.** The Vyas, Eisenstein, Jones 2020 NEJM review documents *clinical algorithms that explicitly use race as an input variable*. eGFR adjusted by Black race produced higher estimated kidney function for Black patients, delaying nephrology referral and transplant listing. VBAC success calculators adjusted by Black/Hispanic race produced lower predicted success rates, steering more women to cesarean. These corrections were introduced without strong biological mechanism and have been progressively removed since 2020.

When evaluating any race-corrected algorithm, ask: **what biological mechanism is "race" actually standing in for?** If the answer is well grounded, the correction may be defensible. If the answer is hand-wavy, the correction is doing the work of structural inequities under a biological label.

### 4. Deployment context: a model is only valid where it was tested

A model that performed well in development may perform poorly when:

- Deployed at an institution different from the one it was developed at.
- Deployed in a workflow different from the one used at validation.
- Deployed across a demographic the training set under-represented.
- Deployed with input data quality different from the development data.
- Deployed during a regime change (a pandemic, a new EHR rollout, a major guideline shift).

Validation studies report performance on the validation cohort. They cannot guarantee performance on a deployment cohort that the validation did not represent. The standard ML term is **distribution shift**. The clinical version is "would this model still work here, now, on these patients?"

### 5. Feedback loops

Once a model is deployed, its predictions shape future clinical decisions. Those decisions generate the data that may be used to retrain the model. The model's own behavior becomes part of its training distribution. This is a feedback loop, and it concentrates bias rather than dissipating it.

A simple example: a sepsis-alert model predicts deterioration. Clinicians act on the alerts. Patients who would have deteriorated without intervention now do not. If the model is retrained on the resulting data, the strong predictors that drove the alerts now appear less strongly associated with deterioration, because the deterioration was prevented. The retrained model down-weights those features and becomes a worse alert.

With bias added: if a model systematically under-alerts on a subgroup (because that subgroup was under-represented in training), that subgroup deteriorates more. The retrained model sees them as a higher-deterioration population, but the model's actual *alert performance* on them remains poor. The disparity is invisible in aggregate metrics and visible only when stratified by subgroup.

## Obermeyer et al. 2019: the canonical case

Published in *Science* in October 2019. The most-cited demonstration of label bias in clinical AI.

### What the algorithm did

A commercial population-health-management algorithm, used across the United States to flag patients for additional care management resources, ranked patients by a "risk score." Patients above a threshold were enrolled in care management programs.

The label the algorithm was trained on was **healthcare costs**, on the theory that costlier patients are sicker patients.

### What the researchers found

The researchers compared the algorithm's outputs against actual measures of illness (active chronic conditions, recent hospitalizations). They found that at any given risk score, **Black patients were considerably sicker than White patients with the same score**. Specifically, the algorithm assigned the same risk score to a Black patient with 26.3% more chronic conditions than the comparable White patient.

The mechanism is the proxy. Black patients receive lower healthcare spending than White patients with equivalent illness severity, due to a combination of access barriers, discrimination, and structural racism. The algorithm learned "low cost = healthy," which means "Black = healthy" relative to equivalently sick White patients. The algorithm was therefore mis-identifying who needed care.

### The fix

The researchers reformulated the label: instead of predicting "cost," predict "active chronic conditions." Retraining on this label, the percentage of Black patients flagged for care management programs would rise from 17.7% to 46.5%.

### The general lesson

The most insidious bias in clinical algorithms is not in features. It is in labels. A bad proxy can be statistically learnable, well-calibrated against itself, and ethically catastrophic. The question "what is the model learning to predict?" must be matched against "what do you actually care about?" If they are not the same thing, the algorithm cannot do what its developers think it does.

This pattern recurs constantly. Any algorithm whose label is cost, length of stay, readmission, billing code, or any other administratively collected outcome is at risk of label bias in the same direction.

## Vyas, Eisenstein, Jones 2020: race correction

Published in *NEJM* in August 2020. A review of clinical algorithms that explicitly include "race" as an input variable, with the question: what biological mechanism is race actually standing in for, and is the mechanism real?

### Examples documented

The article reviews many clinical algorithms; representative ones:

- **eGFR (MDRD and CKD-EPI 2009).** The race-adjusted equations produced higher estimated kidney function for Black patients than for non-Black patients with identical creatinine values. The justification was that Black patients have, on average, higher muscle mass (more creatinine for any given GFR). The mechanism was thin; the consequence was delayed nephrology referral, delayed transplant listing, and delayed initiation of dialysis for Black patients with kidney disease. Removed from major guidelines in 2021.
- **ASCVD risk calculator.** The 2013 Pooled Cohort Equations included separate race-specific models, with Black race generally associated with higher risk. The downstream effect was to push more Black patients into statin therapy. The clinical implications are contested; the mechanism (whether race-specific equations capture biology, environment, or measurement bias) is unclear.
- **VBAC success calculator.** Used to predict success of vaginal birth after cesarean. The race correction produced lower predicted success rates for Black and Hispanic women, steering more of them to repeat cesarean. The mechanism was not biologically grounded. Removed from the calculator in 2021.
- **Lung function reference equations.** Spirometry interpretation used race-specific reference values, with Black patients having lower "normal" values. The mechanism (whether lung capacity differs by race for genetic reasons, or for environmental ones such as differential exposure to pollutants) is contested. The downstream effect was under-diagnosis of lung disease in Black patients.

### The general lesson

Race correction in clinical algorithms requires a clear biological mechanism that the race variable is standing in for. Most documented race corrections in the Vyas review do not have such a mechanism. They were introduced to fit historical data and have downstream effects on referral, treatment, and resource allocation that disadvantage minority patients.

Any time an algorithm uses race as a feature, ask: **what is the mechanism, is it biological, and is the downstream effect ethically defensible?** The honest answer is often that race is doing the work of structural inequities under a biological label.

Since 2020, multiple specialty societies (nephrology, obstetrics, pulmonology, hematology) have removed or revised race-corrected algorithms. The cleanup is ongoing.

## The pulse oximeter case

Not a learned ML model. A clinical device. Same structural failure.

### What the literature shows

Sjoding, Dickson, Iwashyna, Gay, and Valley, *NEJM*, December 2020. The team compared pulse-oximeter readings against simultaneous arterial blood gas measurements in two cohorts. Among patients with pulse oximetry of 92-96%, **Black patients had occult hypoxemia (true SpO2 < 88%) at three times the rate of White patients**. The pulse oximeter was systematically overestimating oxygen saturation in Black patients in clinically consequential ranges.

The mechanism is well-understood: pulse oximetry uses light absorption through the skin. Melanin in darker skin absorbs more of the relevant wavelengths, biasing the calculation. The devices were calibrated and validated on predominantly light-skinned populations.

### Why this case matters for AI bias

The pulse oximeter is not a "model" in the ML sense. It is a transduction of physical light absorption. But the failure pattern is the same as the Obermeyer case and the Vyas cases: a tool validated on one population, deployed without question on another, with bias built in at the moment of validation that is invisible until someone stratifies the performance by subgroup.

During COVID, this case had practical consequences: Black patients with worse true oxygenation than their displayed SpO2 suggested were under-prioritized for supplemental oxygen, hospital admission, and intensive care. The bias was documented in 2020 but had been observed in smaller studies for decades.

### The general lesson

Validation cohort representativeness is a fairness question, even for non-ML tools. Every clinical algorithm, device, or decision rule that was validated on a non-representative population is suspect on the population it was not validated on, until proven otherwise.

## Fairness metrics and the impossibility result

There are several reasonable mathematical definitions of "the model is fair." Some of the most-used:

- **Demographic parity.** The model predicts positive at the same rate across groups. (`P(predicted=+ | A=group_1) = P(predicted=+ | A=group_2)`.)
- **Equal opportunity.** The model's true positive rate (sensitivity) is equal across groups. Patients who *should* be flagged are flagged at the same rate regardless of group.
- **Equalized odds.** Both true positive rate and false positive rate are equal across groups.
- **Calibration.** Predictions correspond to actual probabilities the same way across groups: a 30% risk score means a 30% rate of the outcome in both groups.
- **Predictive parity.** Positive predictive value (probability of the outcome given a positive prediction) is equal across groups.

These look reasonable in isolation. The hard fact:

**Kleinberg, Mullainathan, and Raghavan (arXiv 2016) proved that any non-trivial model cannot satisfy all of (calibration), (equal false positive rate across groups), and (equal false negative rate across groups) simultaneously, unless the base rates of the outcome are equal across groups.**

This is an *impossibility result*. When groups have different base rates of the outcome (which they almost always do in clinical data, often because of differential disease prevalence, differential access to prior care, or differential exposure to risk factors), satisfying one fairness criterion necessarily means violating another.

There is no value-neutral fairness metric. The choice of which criterion to optimize is a value judgment. The choice should be made deliberately and named explicitly.

### What to do with this

- **Pick a fairness criterion appropriate to the use case.** A screening tool where missed cases are dangerous: prioritize equal opportunity (equal sensitivity). A resource-allocation tool: prioritize predictive parity. A diagnostic tool: prioritize calibration.
- **Always stratify performance.** Aggregate AUC means little if subgroup AUCs differ. Report sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and calibration *by subgroup*. The interactive in the notebook lets you see this.
- **Document the trade-off.** The reason for choosing one criterion over another should be in the methods section, the model card, the deployment plan. Hiding the trade-off does not make it go away.

## The seven questions to ask of any model

A practical checklist. Apply this to any vendor pitch, any published model, any CDS tool.

1. **What is the label?** And what is the label actually a proxy for? Is the proxy biased?
2. **What's the training population?** Who is in it, and who is missing? Where was the data collected?
3. **What features are inputs?** Do any of them encode protected characteristics indirectly? Is "race" an explicit feature, and if so, what mechanism is it standing in for?
4. **Has subgroup performance been reported?** Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, calibration, by race, sex, age, insurance status, language, site of care. If only aggregate metrics are reported, that is itself a finding.
5. **Where will this be deployed?** Is the deployment cohort represented in the training and validation cohorts? What is the institution's plan for monitoring deployment performance?
6. **What fairness criterion is being optimized?** Demographic parity, equal opportunity, calibration, predictive parity. The choice should be deliberate and explicit.
7. **What is the post-deployment monitoring plan?** How will the institution know if the model is degrading on a subgroup? Who is responsible for the corrective action?

If a vendor cannot answer five of these seven, the conversation is not ready for the deployment phase.

## Scope of this track

This track is not a course on the mathematics of fairness. The formal literature is extensive (Hardt, Price, Srebro 2016; Chouldechova 2017; Pleiss et al. 2017) and is in the recommended reading. This track is the conceptual frame and the clinical examples. It is not a critique of clinical AI in general. Most clinical AI tools deployed responsibly are improvements over the status quo. The framework here is for *evaluating* them, not for dismissing them. It is not the last word on equity in clinical informatics. Bias has many sources. This track is the algorithmic-systems version. The institutional governance version is Track 05. The data-collection version is Track 03. The privacy-and-surveillance version is Track 01.

Track 05 picks up governance: which committees, which voices, and which decision rights actually decide whether a clinical algorithm gets deployed at your institution and how it gets monitored.
