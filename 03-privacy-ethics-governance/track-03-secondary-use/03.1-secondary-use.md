## Primary use versus secondary use

**Primary use** of clinical data is the use the data was collected for: caring for the patient who produced it. The CRP was drawn to monitor her RA. The diagnosis was coded to bill the encounter. The note was written to document the visit. Each datum exists because a clinical workflow needed it.

**Secondary use** is everything else. Most of modern clinical informatics, including most of this curriculum, is secondary use. The categories that come up in practice are:

- **Quality improvement** (using the data to evaluate and improve care delivery).
- **Healthcare operations** (financial reporting, utilization analysis, payer activities).
- **Public health surveillance** (state reporting, infectious-disease tracking).
- **Academic research** (publications, hypothesis testing, cohort studies).
- **Translational research** (linking observational data to biomarker discovery, biobank-coupled analysis).
- **AI/ML model training** (using the data to build predictive models).
- **Commercial product development** (vendor analytics, pharma partnerships, marketed AI tools).
- **Public benchmark releases** (datasets shared at conferences, used to evaluate community algorithms).

A row in a CRP table can pass through several of these in succession. The patient consented to none of them at the moment of the blood draw.

## Why "legal" and "ethical" do not coincide

Track 02 walked HIPAA and its layers. HIPAA permits a lot. The TPO (Treatment, Payment, Operations) carve-out permits very broad secondary use within the covered entity, without patient authorization. Healthcare operations include "quality assessment and improvement activities," "developing clinical guidelines," "business planning," and "general administrative activities." Most of what a hospital's research and data-science arm does fits one of those categories.

HIPAA was written in 1996 and updated since. It anticipates a world in which clinical data is used by the institution that collected it. It does not anticipate a world in which:

- The institution sells de-identified extracts to a pharmaceutical company for drug development.
- The institution licenses its data to a vendor that resells it across a hundred sites.
- The institution partners with a tech company whose business model is monetizing analysis of those records.
- A model trained on those records is deployed back into the institution's clinical workflow with the vendor brand attached.

Each of these may be HIPAA-compliant. Each violates what most patients would assume when they signed the Notice of Privacy Practices at intake. The legal framework permits the activity. The social contract is, in many cases, broken anyway. The case studies in the next section make this concrete.

## Three landmark cases

### 1. Henrietta Lacks and HeLa (1951 to present)

Henrietta Lacks was a Black woman with cervical cancer who was treated at Johns Hopkins in 1951. During her treatment, a researcher took a sample of her tumor without her knowledge or consent (which was the norm at the time). The cells from that sample (HeLa) turned out to have the unprecedented property of growing indefinitely in culture. They became, and remain, the most widely used cell line in biomedical research. They underpin the polio vaccine, in vitro fertilization, the mapping of the human genome, dozens of Nobel Prize-winning discoveries, and a multi-billion-dollar biotech industry.

Lacks's family did not learn of HeLa's existence for two decades. They received no compensation, no recognition, and no say in how the cells were used. The cells continue to be commercialized today.

**The lesson.** Consent practices in 1951 were not what they are today, and modern IRBs would not permit what was done. The case is still load-bearing. It shows what happens when data (or biological material) outlives the moment of collection, accrues enormous value, and the people whose bodies produced it have no standing in the resulting decisions. The 2013 NIH agreement with the Lacks family gave the family input into research access requests for HeLa whole-genome sequencing data. That was sixty-two years late.

### 2. The Havasupai Diabetes Project (1990s to 2010)

The Havasupai Tribe of northern Arizona consented to blood collection for type 2 diabetes research at Arizona State University. The diabetes research was conducted, but the same samples were also used (without specific consent) for studies of schizophrenia, inbreeding, and migration patterns. Some of these studies produced findings the Tribe found offensive (the migration findings contradicted Tribal religious narratives about origin); all of them used Tribal members' samples for purposes those members had not agreed to.

In 2010, after years of litigation, ASU returned the blood samples and paid the Tribe a $700,000 settlement.

**The lesson.** Consent is not a blank check, and broader research use of a specific-purpose collection is not automatically legitimate. The Havasupai did not object to research. They objected to research they had not been asked about, conducted on samples they had given for a different purpose. The broad-consent provisions in the Revised Common Rule are partly a response to this case and others like it. They try to define what blanket "future research" consent does and does not authorize.

### 3. Project Nightingale (Google + Ascension, 2019)

Ascension, a large Catholic hospital system, partnered with Google to migrate Ascension's data infrastructure to Google Cloud. The arrangement (later branded Project Nightingale) gave Google access to the medical records of tens of millions of patients across 21 states for the purpose of building software to assist Ascension's clinicians.

The partnership was disclosed in a Wall Street Journal investigation, not in a public announcement. Patients were not told. Ascension and Google argued the arrangement was a Business Associate relationship under HIPAA (Google was processing PHI on Ascension's behalf, which is permitted with a Business Associate Agreement) and was therefore legally compliant. They were broadly correct.

The public response was sharply negative. The case became a textbook example of HIPAA-compliant practice that nonetheless violated the social contract. Patients did not know Google was looking at their records. They did not know what Google was building. They did not know what Google was learning that might end up in Google's other products. The legal framework permitted the partnership. The trust framework did not survive the disclosure.

**The lesson.** The category of "Business Associate" was written in 1996 to cover the billing service and the transcription company. It now covers cloud-platform partnerships that fundamentally restructure who has access to which patient records, with what tools, for which purposes. The legal definition has not been updated. The practical implications have changed substantially.

## Consent, broad consent, and waivers

### What patients actually consent to

A patient signs three to five forms at most outpatient intake visits:

- The **Notice of Privacy Practices** acknowledgment, which describes, in dense legal language that most patients do not read, what the institution may do with their PHI under HIPAA TPO and what they need authorization for.
- A **financial responsibility** agreement.
- A **treatment consent** for the planned care.
- Possibly a **research consent** (one or more) for specific protocols.
- Possibly a **broad consent** for "future research" use of leftover specimens or data.

Most patients reasonably understand the first three. The fourth is study-specific and well-bounded by the protocol. The fifth (broad consent) is the contested one. The Revised Common Rule (2018) added explicit provisions for broad consent: a patient can give blanket consent for future, unspecified research use of identifiable data and specimens, provided the consent describes the categories of research and the institution honors it.

Broad consent buys flexibility for researchers and reduces re-consent burden. It can also build patient trust over time *if* the institution honors the categories the patient was told about and *if* the categories are described honestly. The Havasupai case shows what happens when the second condition is not met.

### When IRBs grant waivers

An IRB can waive the requirement for individual consent for research using PHI when four conditions are satisfied:

1. The research involves no more than minimal risk to subjects.
2. The waiver will not adversely affect the rights and welfare of subjects.
3. The research could not practicably be carried out without the waiver.
4. Where appropriate, subjects will be provided with additional pertinent information after participation.

These criteria are tight on paper. In practice, they are met routinely for retrospective EHR-based research, because individually consenting tens of thousands of past patients is not practicable. The waiver is the default operational path for secondary-use research on existing data.

The waiver is a *trade*. The research could not happen without it, and the patients could not realistically have been asked. The trade only holds if the research is genuinely valuable to the patients (or to a group that includes the patients) and does not expose them to harm. The IRB enforces the trade procedurally. The ethical defensibility depends on whether the trade was honest.

## The five dimensions of ethical risk

A framework for evaluating any proposed secondary use. None of these dimensions are binary. Each is a continuum the project lives somewhere on.

### 1. Consent-expectation fit

Does the proposed use match what a reasonable patient would have expected when they consented to care? An internal QI project on sepsis bundle compliance fits intuitively. A pharma deal for drug development does not. The gap between these is the expectation-fit dimension.

Operationally: if you described the proposed use to a sample of the patients whose data is involved, what fraction would say "that's what I thought my data was for?" The honest answer is rarely 100%.

### 2. Public vs commercial benefit balance

Where does the benefit accrue? A public-health study published openly benefits the public. A model trained on patient data and sold commercially benefits the company and its customers. The same dataset can support both, but the framing matters: who is the beneficiary?

Commercial is not automatically bad. Drug development is commercial, and new drugs benefit patients. But the framing should be honest. A "research collaboration" that produces a proprietary commercial product is a different kind of secondary use than a "research collaboration" that produces a peer-reviewed paper, and the consent and disclosure expectations differ.

### 3. Equity

Whose data is being used, and whose interests does the use serve?

Three equity questions:

- **Inclusion equity.** Is the dataset representative of the population that will benefit from the work?
- **Burden equity.** Are the costs (privacy risk, opportunity costs) distributed fairly?
- **Benefit equity.** Will the results help the population the data came from?

A model trained predominantly on data from white patients in academic medical centers, then deployed in safety-net hospitals serving predominantly Black patients, fails all three. The Havasupai case fails all three. The Henrietta Lacks case fails all three.

### 4. Transparency

Could a patient find out what's happening with their data if they wanted to?

The Project Nightingale case scored low here. The partnership was not disclosed publicly until journalists revealed it. The legal framework did not require disclosure (HIPAA TPO permits it, and the BAA arrangement does not trigger a notification requirement). The trust framework, in retrospect, did require disclosure, and the failure to provide it produced the backlash.

Transparency can be at multiple levels: institutional disclosure, individual notice, ongoing reporting. The right level depends on the use. The wrong level is none.

### 5. Reversibility

Can a patient withdraw their data from the use if they object?

For broad-consent collections, the patient typically has the right to withdraw (with limits on data already used). For waiver-of-consent research, the patient often cannot withdraw, because the research is using data they did not consent to in the first place. For data sold to a commercial partner under a DUA, reversibility depends on the DUA terms. In most cases, the partner has the right to retain extracts. For data used to train a deployed AI model, the model often cannot be untrained.

Low reversibility is not automatically wrong. Some research questions need the data to be locked in. But low reversibility increases the importance of every other dimension, because the patient has no recourse if any of those dimensions is violated.

## Commercial vs academic vs translational

The destination matters. A few common archetypes:

| Archetype | What it looks like | Typical ethical risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal QI** | Operations using existing data to improve care delivery at the same institution | Low across dimensions when scope is clear |
| **Academic publication** | Researcher publishes a paper using de-identified institutional data, IRB-approved | Low to medium; the question is whether the research benefits the patient population |
| **Cross-institutional research network** | TriNetX, OHDSI, AllOfUs: standardized data across sites, used by registered investigators | Medium; depends on the governance structure and patient consent process |
| **Public benchmark release** | A de-identified dataset shared at a conference for community algorithm development | Medium; once released, you can't recall it. Equity in who can use it matters. |
| **Pharma research partnership** | Pharmaceutical company pays the institution for data access for drug development | Medium-high; commercial benefit framing requires explicit disclosure |
| **Vendor analytics partnership** | Cloud platform or AI vendor uses institutional data for product development | High; the vendor's other customers may benefit from learnings derived from this institution's patients |
| **Model deployment with vendor branding** | A model trained on institutional data is sold back as a product | High; the patients whose data trained it have no stake in its continued use |

The framework is not meant to rule out the high-risk archetypes. The high-risk archetypes need to carry proportionate disclosure, consent, governance, and benefit-sharing.

## Scope of this track

This track is not arguing to stop doing secondary use. Most of medicine's progress in the last fifty years has depended on it. It is not arguing that every patient must explicitly consent to every use. That would be unworkable and would shut down secondary research, which most patients want to happen. It is not arguing that legal frameworks are useless. HIPAA, IRBs, and consent processes are the operational scaffolding. The ethical work happens on top of the scaffolding, not in place of it. The track is a working vocabulary for the conversation, not a substitute for an ethicist.

Track 04 picks up algorithmic fairness, where most of the equity dimensions from this track surface most sharply. Track 05 closes with governance: who decides what gets approved, and what the clinician's role is in that decision.
