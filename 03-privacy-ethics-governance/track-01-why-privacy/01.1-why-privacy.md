## What is at stake

A clinician's intuition for patient privacy is shaped by the bedside: do not discuss the patient in the elevator, log out of the chart, close the curtain. The intuition is correct. The mechanism is different at scale.

When clinical data leaves the bedside (an extract for a researcher, a dataset for a vendor, a public-use file for a federal agency), the privacy question changes from "who can overhear" to "who can re-identify." Re-identification means taking a dataset stripped of obvious identifiers and recovering the link from rows back to specific individuals. Real people with real medical records live behind every row. A leak is not abstract. It ends careers, marriages, and insurance coverage.

The attacks are concrete and the failure modes are well documented. This track walks the documented failures.

---

## The threat model

Three questions get you most of the way:

**Who is the attacker?** In documented re-identification cases, the attackers have been journalists looking for a public figure, researchers demonstrating a vulnerability, governments doing surveillance, employers checking on staff, and (less often) commercial entities cross-referencing data brokers' tables. The threat is not someone hacking your firewall. The threat is someone with a side table you did not know existed.

**Why would they bother?** Curiosity (who is in this dataset), demonstration (proof that re-identification is feasible), commercial interest (linking a medical record to a marketing profile), litigation (finding a plaintiff or witness), or harm (outing, blackmail, denying insurance). Most documented re-identifications were demonstrations by researchers. The rest were a mix.

**What would re-identification let them do?** Connect the dataset's medical content (diagnoses, prescriptions, procedures, dates) to a specific named person. Once that link is made, the content becomes leverage. The leverage may be reputational (a public figure's diagnosis), financial (a person's insurance status), or legal (a witness's address).

Most clinical-research datasets are exposed to all three motivations. The defenses scale with them.

## The three famous re-identifications

### 1. Governor Weld's medical records (Sweeney, 1997)

In the mid-1990s, the Massachusetts Group Insurance Commission released "anonymized" hospital discharge records for state employees. They removed name, address, Social Security number. They kept ZIP code, date of birth, and sex.

Latanya Sweeney, then a graduate student at MIT, paid twenty dollars for the publicly available voter registration list of Cambridge, Massachusetts. She matched the discharge records to that list on the three remaining fields. The Governor of Massachusetts at the time, William Weld, lived in Cambridge. His ZIP code, date of birth, and sex appeared in both files. She mailed his medical record to his office.

The obvious identifiers were removed, the data was called anonymized, and a single match against a publicly purchasable side table re-identified an individual record. The three fields that did it (ZIP, sex, DOB) are what we now call **quasi-identifiers**. Each one is harmless on its own. Together they are nearly unique.

### 2. The Netflix Prize dataset (Narayanan and Shmatikov, 2008)

In 2006, Netflix released a dataset of 100 million movie ratings from 500,000 subscribers, with subscriber names replaced by random IDs, as a public competition to improve their recommendation algorithm.

Two years later, Arvind Narayanan and Vitaly Shmatikov at the University of Texas published a paper showing that with as few as eight movie ratings (some of which could be slightly wrong, and some of which could be off by up to two weeks in date), they could uniquely identify a Netflix subscriber in the dataset 99% of the time. They cross-referenced against the Internet Movie Database, where some users had publicly posted reviews.

The attack worked because of **the high dimensionality of sparse data**. If you watched a hundred movies and someone knows your reviews of eight obscure ones, the probability that any other subscriber has the same eight movies is essentially zero. The dataset did not contain names, but the *pattern* of ratings was itself an identifier.

A series of diagnoses, lab values, or prescriptions can play the same role as a sequence of obscure movies. The more unusual the clinical history, the easier to re-identify. Patients with rare diseases or complex courses are systematically more identifiable than patients with common conditions.

### 3. The AOL search query release (2006)

In 2006, AOL released 20 million search queries from 657,000 users for academic research, with usernames replaced by integer IDs. Within days, journalists at the New York Times re-identified User 4417749 by reading her queries, which included her family name's last initial, her hometown, and the names of people she had searched for. They visited her and asked if she was the user behind those queries. She was. AOL pulled the dataset four days after release, but copies are still in circulation.

Even when the dataset is text only (no demographics, no ZIP, no DOB), the *content* people produce is itself identifying. The clinical analog is the free-text note, which Track 10 of this curriculum picks up.

## De-identification versus anonymization

These two words are often used interchangeably. They mean different things.

**De-identification** is the removal of *direct identifiers*: name, address, Social Security number, MRN, account numbers. Under HIPAA's Safe Harbor (Track 02 territory), there are eighteen specific identifier categories that must be removed for a dataset to qualify. After de-identification, the dataset is legally permitted to be shared under specific conditions in the United States.

**Anonymization** is the property that re-identification is statistically infeasible regardless of what side tables an attacker may have. There is no universal threshold for "anonymized" the way there is for "de-identified." Reasonable approaches use formal privacy models (k-anonymity, l-diversity, t-closeness, differential privacy), each of which makes a different guarantee.

In practice, almost every "anonymized" or "de-identified" clinical dataset in circulation is de-identified, not anonymized. The eighteen Safe Harbor identifiers are removed. The quasi-identifiers (ZIP, DOB, dates of service, sex, race, ethnicity) remain. The dataset is legally shareable but not statistically un-re-identifiable.

This is not necessarily wrong. Safe Harbor trades privacy for utility. Some research questions need dates of service. The HIPAA framework's answer is to require a Data Use Agreement and an IRB review (both Track 02) before sharing such a dataset. The vocabulary that distinguishes "de-identified" from "anonymized" matters because the protections differ in kind, not in degree alone.

## The quasi-identifier problem and k-anonymity

A **quasi-identifier** is a field that is not identifying on its own but becomes identifying in combination with other fields. The clinical quasi-identifiers worth knowing:

- ZIP code (especially full 5-digit), date of birth, sex (the Sweeney trio).
- Date of admission, date of discharge, date of service (combine narrowly with diagnosis or hospital).
- Race and ethnicity in small populations.
- Rare diagnoses (a 28-year-old with Castleman disease in a 5,000-row dataset is identifiable on the diagnosis alone).
- Sequence-of-encounter patterns (the timing and order of admissions in a longitudinal dataset).

The standard quantitative defense is **k-anonymity** (Sweeney, 2002). A dataset is *k-anonymous* on a set of quasi-identifiers if every row's combination of those fields is shared by at least *k* other rows in the dataset. A 3-anonymous dataset means no row's quasi-identifiers can be linked to fewer than 3 candidates. A 100-anonymous dataset is much harder to attack.

The standard defenses to *achieve* k-anonymity:

- **Generalization.** Replace 5-digit ZIP with 3-digit ZIP. Replace exact date of birth with year. Replace exact date of admission with month.
- **Suppression.** For rows whose quasi-identifiers are nearly unique, blank out one of the fields rather than release them as is.
- **Aggregation.** Release counts by groups rather than individual rows.

K-anonymity is a floor, not a ceiling. Two refinements:

- **l-diversity** (Machanavajjhala et al., 2007). Even if no row is uniquely identifiable, every group of k similar rows should contain at least *l* different sensitive values. Otherwise an attacker who can identify the group can infer the sensitive value.
- **Differential privacy** (Dwork, 2006). A mathematical guarantee that the output of an analysis does not depend meaningfully on whether any single individual's record is in the database. The strongest formal model, used in the US Census and increasingly in clinical research releases.

## Scope of this track

This track is not arguing that all clinical data should be locked up. Research depends on shared data, patients depend on the research, and the privacy work is to do that sharing carefully. It is not arguing that de-identification is a fraud. It is a legal framework with known limits, and knowing the limits is the point. It does not require you to be a privacy mathematician. Differential privacy is the deep version. The working clinician's version is to understand the threat model, recognize the quasi-identifier problem, and know when to ask for help.

Track 02 picks up the legal framework (HIPAA Safe Harbor and Expert Determination). Track 03 takes the ethics of secondary use of clinical data. Track 04 connects privacy thinking to algorithmic fairness. Track 05 closes with governance: who is in the room when these decisions get made.
