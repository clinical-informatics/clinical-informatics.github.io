# Governance structures

A reference companion to the notebook. Read it first, after, or skip it.

## Decisions get made somewhere

Tracks 01 through 04 built the analytic frameworks. The threat model says what we are defending against. The legal framework says what the floor is. The ethical framework says what the floor is missing. The equity framework says what bias looks like and how it gets baked in.

None of those frameworks deploys itself. Someone has to decide that *this specific project* gets approved, *this specific dataset* gets shared, *this specific model* gets pushed into production. In most institutions, that someone is a committee. The committee is the unit of governance. This track covers how the committees work, what each one decides, who sits on them, and where the clinician's voice belongs.

---

## The map of governance bodies

A modern academic medical center has many committees that touch clinical-data projects. The relevant ones, in roughly the order a project might encounter them:

### IRB (Institutional Review Board)

**What it decides.** Whether a proposed activity is research; whether the research is exempt, expedited, or full-board; whether informed consent can be waived; whether the privacy safeguards are adequate; whether vulnerable populations are protected.

**Who sits.** Faculty researchers, clinicians, ethicists, biostatisticians, community representatives (one or more "unaffiliated members" required by the Common Rule), legal counsel.

**Authority.** Federal regulatory authority for human-subjects research (45 CFR 46 / Common Rule; 21 CFR for FDA-regulated studies). The IRB's "no" is hard to overturn.

**Limitation.** The IRB reviews research. It does not review quality improvement, healthcare operations, vendor deployments outside a research protocol, or commercial partnerships. Many clinical-data projects fall outside the IRB's scope and need other governance.

### Privacy office

**What it decides.** Whether a proposed use of PHI is HIPAA-compliant; whether a de-identification approach (Safe Harbor or Expert Determination) is adequate; what Data Use Agreements need to be in place; what Business Associate Agreements are required.

**Who sits.** Privacy officer (typically reports to compliance or the general counsel), privacy analysts, legal counsel.

**Authority.** Operational authority within the institution; can stop or modify projects on HIPAA grounds. The state privacy regulators and HHS Office for Civil Rights are the external authorities the privacy office is accountable to.

**Limitation.** The privacy office is necessary but not sufficient for ethical review. HIPAA-compliant is not the same as ethically defensible (Track 03).

### Compliance office

**What it decides.** Whether the institution is meeting its regulatory obligations across all dimensions (privacy, security, billing, fraud, anti-kickback, Stark, research integrity, etc.). Whether contracts comply with applicable law. Whether the institution's policies are being followed.

**Who sits.** Chief compliance officer, compliance analysts, often the institution's legal counsel.

**Authority.** Operational authority; can stop projects that violate regulatory or institutional policy.

**Limitation.** Compliance is about following rules. The rules can be wrong; compliance won't catch that. Most ethical questions are out of scope.

### Data governance committee

**What it decides.** Who is permitted to access institutional data, for what purposes; what data stewardship looks like; how data quality is managed; what extracts can leave the institution.

**Who sits.** Chief Data Officer or equivalent; data stewards from each major source system (EHR, billing, lab, imaging); informatics leadership; compliance representation; sometimes a research representative.

**Authority.** Operational authority over institutional data assets. Varies widely by institution.

**Limitation.** The data governance committee tends to focus on stewardship (who can touch what data) more than on downstream use (what gets built with it). A project can pass the data governance committee and still raise the ethical issues of Track 03 or the equity issues of Track 04.

### AI/ML governance committee

**What it decides.** Whether a proposed AI/ML tool can be developed at the institution, validated, or deployed in clinical workflows; whether the tool's performance is monitored post-deployment; whether the tool meets equity standards; whether a vendor partnership for AI/ML is acceptable.

**Who sits.** This committee is the newest in most institutions and varies most by site. Common members: a clinical informatics lead; a data scientist; a clinician (often the institutional CMIO or AI champion); compliance/legal; sometimes a patient representative.

**Authority.** Varies. Some institutions give the AI governance committee binding authority over clinical AI deployments. Others give it advisory authority only.

**Limitation.** Many AI tools enter institutions through other doors (vendor procurement, departmental decisions, "we already had this contract") and may bypass the AI governance committee entirely.

### Vendor management / procurement

**What it decides.** Whether a vendor contract can be signed; what terms the contract must contain; whether the vendor's security and privacy posture is adequate; what BAA terms are required.

**Who sits.** Procurement, contract attorneys, IT security, privacy office representation, sometimes the requesting clinical or research department.

**Authority.** Operational authority over institutional contracts. Strong, in principle. Variable in practice.

**Limitation.** Procurement is focused on the contract, not on the clinical workflow the vendor will end up in. A vendor can be properly contracted and still poorly governed in deployment.

### Clinical informatics steering committee (or CMIO's office)

**What it decides.** Whether a proposed change to the EHR or clinical decision support is approved; whether a tool integrates with clinical workflows; whether the change is communicated to end users.

**Who sits.** CMIO, CNIO, informatics directors, lead clinicians from major specialties, IT operations.

**Authority.** Operational authority over the clinical informatics platform. Strong on what gets into the EHR; less direct authority over standalone tools.

**Limitation.** Focuses on the EHR-integrated workflow. Standalone analytics tools, research dashboards, and back-office systems may not pass through this committee.

### Security / CISO office

**What it decides.** Whether a system meets the institution's security requirements; whether data flows are appropriately encrypted, logged, and monitored; whether the vendor's security posture is adequate.

**Who sits.** CISO, security engineers, infrastructure architects.

**Authority.** Operational authority over institutional systems. Strong, especially after a breach.

**Limitation.** Focused on confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data systems. Equity and clinical-appropriateness questions are out of scope.

### Legal / Office of General Counsel

**What it decides.** Whether a contract is enforceable; whether the institution's exposure is acceptable; whether terms align with state, federal, and international law.

**Who sits.** Attorneys.

**Authority.** Strong on contractual terms; advisory on operational decisions.

**Limitation.** Lawyers focus on enforceability and risk. The fact that something is legally defensible doesn't make it operationally good.

## Decision rights: where they get blurry

In principle, the committees above have clearly delineated authority. In practice, **the boundaries are blurry**, and the same project often touches several of them with no single owner.

A concrete example: a proposed pilot of a vendor AI tool for predicting deterioration on the inpatient wards.

- **Is it research?** If yes, IRB review. If no, the answer depends on the framing (QI, operations, deployment study), and the framing determines whether the IRB is involved.
- **Does it touch PHI?** Yes. The privacy office must review.
- **Does it require a contract?** Yes. Vendor management and legal must review.
- **Does it integrate with the EHR?** Probably. Clinical informatics steering must review.
- **Is it an AI tool?** Yes. AI/ML governance must review.
- **Does it affect clinical workflow?** Yes. The CMIO and departmental leadership must approve.
- **Are there equity considerations?** Yes (per Track 04). Who reviews this?

The last question is the most telling. **In most institutions, the equity review does not have an obvious home.** It may live in the AI governance committee if there is one. It may live nowhere if there is not. The same is true for the *ethical* review beyond what HIPAA and the IRB cover. In most institutions, this lives nowhere unless the AI governance committee or a research ethics consultation service has been deliberately set up to handle it.

Some governance questions have no clear owner. Projects either stall (because no one steps up) or proceed (because no one stops them). Both failure modes are common. The fix is to **explicitly name the decision rights** before the project starts: who decides what, on what timeline, with what backstop.

## The clinician's role

Most of the committees above are not staffed by working clinicians. The IRB and the AI governance committee usually have one or two. The privacy office, compliance, vendor management, legal, and security usually do not.

This matters for two reasons:

- **Clinical context is hard to get right without clinicians.** "Does this tool fit the workflow?" "Does this label measure what it claims to measure?" "Does this alert make clinical sense?" These questions require working knowledge of the workflow that the committee may not have.
- **The downstream consequences of governance decisions land on clinicians.** When a tool is deployed and alerts inappropriately, the clinician deals with the alert fatigue. When a vendor partnership locks the institution into a contract that limits future flexibility, the clinician deals with the limitation. When an equity issue is not caught at deployment, the clinician sees the disparate impact in their patients.

**Ask for a seat** on whichever committee touches the work you care about. Most institutions are receptive. Clinicians who volunteer for governance committees are often welcomed, because the committees know they need clinical input.

What to bring to the seat:

1. **Workflow reality.** What the day actually looks like for the clinicians the tool will affect.
2. **Outcome literacy.** What the outcome metric the tool optimizes actually means, and whether it captures what matters.
3. **Subgroup awareness.** Which patients the tool might fail on, and whether anyone has checked.
4. **Communication clarity.** Plain-English translation of clinical questions for the non-clinical committee members.
5. **Skepticism without obstruction.** The committee needs critical input, not a veto. The best clinical representatives say "yes, with these conditions" more often than "no."

## Vendor contracts: where the actual policy lives

A vendor contract for a clinical AI tool runs 60-200 pages. Most committee members have not read it. The contract contains the actual policy that will govern the deployment: what data the vendor can keep, what they can use it for, what happens to model improvements derived from the institution's data, what the institution's recourse is if the vendor fails or is acquired, what notification the institution gets when the model changes.

A representative list of contract terms that matter clinically:

| Term | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Data ownership** | Who owns the records, the derived models, the labeled data, the metadata about clinical use? |
| **Data use limitations** | Can the vendor use this institution's data to improve products sold to other customers? |
| **Model update notification** | If the model is silently updated, does the institution learn about it? Validate against the new version? |
| **Performance monitoring obligations** | Who monitors the model post-deployment? What's the reporting cadence? What triggers a review? |
| **Subgroup performance reporting** | Is the vendor obligated to report stratified performance? At what cadence? With what thresholds? |
| **Termination and offboarding** | If the institution terminates, does the vendor delete the data? Retain extracts? Keep derived models? |
| **Liability and indemnification** | Who is liable for a clinical mis-prediction that causes harm? |
| **Auditing rights** | Can the institution audit the vendor's data handling? On what notice? |
| **Subcontractor flow-down** | Do the vendor's subcontractors also follow these terms? |

The clinician on the governance committee does not need to be a contract lawyer. The clinician does need to ask, on each of these dimensions: "what does our contract say, and is that what we want it to say?" Many vendor contracts are silent on the equity-relevant terms (subgroup performance reporting, model update notification with revalidation requirement). Silence is the default, and the default favors the vendor.

## Patient and community representation

Who speaks for the patients?

The available answers are imperfect:

- **Unaffiliated IRB members.** Required by the Common Rule. One per IRB. Often a single voice across many protocols.
- **Patient and Family Advisory Councils (PFACs).** Common in academic medical centers. Often consulted on workflow and experience questions; less often consulted on data and AI questions.
- **Community advisory boards.** Common in research networks that serve specific communities. The Havasupai case (Track 03) is an example of what community consultation can prevent when it works, and what it cannot when it's bypassed.
- **Patient representatives on AI governance.** Increasing but still uncommon. The few institutions that have done this report it changes the conversation substantively.

The right question is not "is there a patient on the committee" (the answer is often yes, in some form). The right question is "does that voice have actual influence on the decision?" Tokens are common. Influence is rarer.

The institutions doing this best treat patient representation as a real governance role: paid, prepared, briefed in advance, given voting rights, supported with appropriate background materials. The institutions doing it worst treat patient representation as a symbolic checkbox. The difference is visible in retrospect, when the project either has trust to draw on or does not.

## Two case studies

### Project Nightingale (Google + Ascension, 2019): governance failure

Track 03 covered this case from the ethical-framework angle. From the governance angle, the failure is specific:

- The partnership was *contractually* compliant (BAA in place, HIPAA-permitted).
- The partnership was *institutionally* compliant (signed by the relevant authorities).
- The partnership was **not socially disclosed**. Patients were not told. Clinicians were not told. The public was not told.
- The discovery was via Wall Street Journal investigation, not via the institution's own transparency processes.

The governance failure is that no committee was responsible for asking "should this be disclosed?" The legal and contracting committees said yes to the contract. Nobody owned the disclosure decision. Nobody noticed that the BAA framework was being used at a scale and intimacy the BAA was not designed for. The committee structure existed. The decision rights for the *novel* dimension of the partnership did not.

**The lesson.** Governance committees can be individually competent and collectively miss the point. When a project is *new in kind* (the first vendor partnership of its scale, the first model deployment of its type, the first community engagement of its sort), the standing committees may not have anyone whose job it is to think about the novelty. The fix is to explicitly elevate "is anything new about this?" as a governance question, and to require an answer.

### Duke Sepsis Watch: governance done well

Sendak, Gao, Brajer, and Balu et al. published a series of papers (2017-2020) on the development and deployment of Sepsis Watch, an AI-based deterioration prediction tool deployed in production at Duke. What made it a model case for governance:

- **Multi-stakeholder development.** Clinicians, data scientists, and operations people worked jointly on the tool from the start. The clinical leadership was not consulted after the fact.
- **Integration governance.** The tool was integrated into the existing rapid-response workflow, not deployed as a parallel alerting system. The committee responsible for the rapid-response workflow had authority over the integration.
- **Post-deployment monitoring.** The team monitored the tool's performance continuously and reported back to the clinical and administrative leadership. Disparate-impact analyses by race and other subgroups were part of the monitoring plan.
- **Documented decisions.** The deployment plan, the validation results, the monitoring plan, and the modification history were documented in published work.
- **Tied to organizational quality goals.** The tool was operationally accountable to a sepsis mortality reduction target; if the tool's deployment did not move that target, the deployment was at risk of being reconsidered.

Sepsis Watch is not a template (different institutions have different needs). The lesson is that *governance is something you can do well or badly*, and the differences are visible in retrospect. Duke chose to do it well, and the resulting tool has held up.

## Scope of this track

This track is not a list of every committee at every institution. Names, structures, and decision rights vary. The categories above are general. Your local instance differs. It is not advice on how to navigate your specific institution. The advice is to find out, locally, who decides what, and how to be in the room. It is not a substitute for the institutional ethics consultation service. Some institutions have one. If yours does, use it. This track provides the vocabulary. The consultation provides the institutional context.

The capstone for this course brings the threat model (Track 01), the legal framework (Track 02), the ethical framework (Track 03), the algorithmic-fairness framework (Track 04), and the governance framework (this track) into a Socratic review of a proposed research project. The track sequence is the framework. The capstone is the application.
