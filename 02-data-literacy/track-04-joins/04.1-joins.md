A reference companion to the notebook. Read it first, after, or skip it.

## What a join actually is

A **join** takes two tables that share a column and produces a new table where the rows are matched on that column. The shared column is the **join key**.

Concrete example. You have a demographics table and a labs table for an RA cohort:

```
demographics                 labs
patient_id | age | sex       patient_id | date       | crp
-----------+-----+-----      -----------+------------+----
ER-001     | 52  | F         ER-001     | 2024-04-17 | 18.7
ER-002     | 38  | F         ER-001     | 2024-07-22 | 14.1
ER-003     | 67  | M         ER-002     | 2024-05-30 | 11.2
                             ER-002     | 2024-09-04 | 8.6
```

A join on `patient_id` produces:

```
patient_id | age | sex | date       | crp
-----------+-----+-----+------------+----
ER-001     | 52  | F   | 2024-04-17 | 18.7
ER-001     | 52  | F   | 2024-07-22 | 14.1
ER-002     | 38  | F   | 2024-05-30 | 11.2
ER-002     | 38  | F   | 2024-09-04 | 8.6
```

ER-003 has demographics but no labs, so they do not appear. That is a choice the join made. Which choice it made depends on the join type. The four basic types differ only in what they do with the rows that do not match.

---

## Join keys

The join key is the column (or columns) used to match rows. For a join to behave sensibly:

- **The key must mean the same thing in both tables.** Two MRN columns that both say `ER-001` must refer to the same patient. If one table's `patient_id` is from Epic and the other is from a study registry, they may not match even when they look identical.
- **The key must be the same type.** Track 01 territory. A `patient_id` stored as integer in one table and as text in another will not match even when the underlying values are identical.
- **The key should be unique in at least one table** if you want a clean one-to-many result. If both sides have duplicates, you get a many-to-many explosion (Section 5 below).

A **composite key** is multiple columns joined together. To match a specific encounter rather than only a patient, you join on `(patient_id, encounter_date)`. To match a specific lab result, you join on `(patient_id, specimen_date, loinc_code)`. Composite keys are the norm in real clinical data, not the exception.

---

## The four basic joins

Every join is one of these. The result differs only in how unmatched rows are handled.

### Inner join

**Definition.** Keep only the rows where the key appears in *both* tables. Unmatched rows on either side are dropped.

```python
combined = demographics.merge(labs, on="patient_id", how="inner")
```

**When to use.** When you only want patients who appear in both datasets. "Patients in the registry *and* with at least one CRP drawn."

**The trap.** Patients drop silently. ER-003 had demographics but no labs, so the inner join does not include them. If the analysis is "mean CRP across the cohort," the result is the mean of the patients who had a CRP drawn. That is not the same as the mean across the cohort.

### Left join

**Definition.** Keep all rows from the left table. For each left-table row, attach the matching right-table data if it exists; if not, fill with nulls.

```python
combined = demographics.merge(labs, on="patient_id", how="left")
```

**When to use.** When the left table defines the cohort and you want to preserve it. "All 200 cohort patients, with labs where available." ER-003 appears in the result with null CRP, which is honest (they are in the cohort, they do not have labs).

**The note.** Left join followed by a filter on the right side's columns is silently an inner join. `df.merge(labs, how="left").dropna(subset=["crp"])` produces the same result as an inner join, but does not read as "I'm dropping patients."

### Right join

**Definition.** The mirror of left. Keep all rows from the right table; attach matching left-table data if it exists.

```python
combined = demographics.merge(labs, on="patient_id", how="right")
```

**When to use.** Rarely. A right join is a left join with the arguments swapped, and the convention is to swap and use left. Right joins exist for symmetry. In practice you almost never see them in clinical code.

### Full outer join

**Definition.** Keep all rows from both tables. Unmatched rows on either side fill with nulls.

```python
combined = demographics.merge(labs, on="patient_id", how="outer")
```

**When to use.** When you want the union: every patient who appears in either table. Useful for data reconciliation: "show me every patient who is in demographics or labs or both, and flag which side they came from." Less useful for analysis, because most analyses want to know *which* patients are represented, not all of them.

### Anti-join

**Definition.** Not a standard `merge` option, but a critical conceptual operation. Returns the rows from one table whose keys do *not* appear in the other.

```python
# Patients in demographics but missing from labs:
in_demographics_only = demographics[~demographics["patient_id"].isin(labs["patient_id"])]

# Patients in labs but missing from demographics:
in_labs_only = labs[~labs["patient_id"].isin(demographics["patient_id"])]
```

**Why it matters.** The anti-join is how you measure silent patient loss. Before you publish "we analyzed 153 patients," you should know how many patients dropped out at each join step, and why. The anti-join is the diagnostic.

---

## The silent patient loss problem

The most common cohort-shrinkage scenario, exhibited:

```python
# Step 1: load demographics. 200 patients.
demographics = pd.read_csv("demographics.csv")
len(demographics)  # 200

# Step 2: join to labs. Some patients had no labs in the export.
df = demographics.merge(labs, on="patient_id", how="inner")
len(df["patient_id"].unique())  # 178. 22 patients dropped at this step.

# Step 3: join to medications. Some patients have demographics + labs but no med records.
df = df.merge(medications, on="patient_id", how="inner")
len(df["patient_id"].unique())  # 142. Another 36 patients gone.

# Step 4: join to outcomes.
df = df.merge(outcomes, on="patient_id", how="inner")
len(df["patient_id"].unique())  # 117. The "analysis cohort" is 117, not 200.
```

The mean of any outcome computed over `df` is the mean of the patients who survived every join step. It is not the mean of the 200-patient cohort. The 83 patients lost may differ from the 117 kept in clinically meaningful ways: sicker, healthier, more recent, less recent, from a specific site, on Medicare, the list is long.

**The fix is not to avoid inner joins.** The fix is to *measure the loss at every step* and decide whether each drop is acceptable. The anti-join is the measurement tool:

```python
demographics_no_labs = demographics[~demographics["patient_id"].isin(labs["patient_id"])]
print(f"Lost at lab-join step: {len(demographics_no_labs)} patients")
print(demographics_no_labs.describe())  # Are they systematically different?
```

If the 22 lost patients are evenly distributed across age, sex, and recruitment date, the loss is plausibly random. If they are all 80+ patients from one site recruited in the last six months, the loss is structured and the analysis is biased. The 80+ patients were probably the ones who could not make follow-up labs. The analysis now overrepresents healthier patients. This is a Track 03 missingness problem disguised as a Track 04 join problem.

## Many-to-many joins and the row explosion

A join on a key that is unique in both tables produces clean one-to-one rows. A join on a key that is unique in one table and repeated in the other (the common case) produces clean one-to-many rows: each "one" gets paired with all its "many." Demographics joined to labs is one-to-many: one demographic row, many labs per patient.

What happens when the key is repeated on *both* sides? The Cartesian product. Every row on the left matches every row on the right with the same key.

```
A: patient_id | drug             B: patient_id | condition
   ER-001     | MTX                 ER-001     | RA
   ER-001     | Adalimumab          ER-001     | Anemia
   ER-001     | Folic acid          ER-001     | HTN
```

Joined on `patient_id`, you get 3 x 3 = 9 rows for ER-001, every drug paired with every condition. That is a row explosion. The output table looks plausible until you notice that ER-001 is now listed three times as taking MTX, and "ER-001 taking MTX for HTN" appears in the result table even though clinically nobody prescribed MTX for HTN.

The clinical version: a cohort of 200 patients, joined to a meds table where each patient has on average 5 meds, joined to a conditions table where each patient has on average 4 conditions. The result has 200 x 5 x 4 = 4,000 rows. A naive `len(df["patient_id"].unique())` shows 200, which is correct. A naive `df["crp"].mean()` is wrong by a factor of (drug-count x condition-count) per patient, because every CRP is counted multiple times in the average.

**The fix is to keep the join's grain explicit.** Before any join, ask: "what is the unique key on each side?" If both sides have duplicates on the key, use a composite key that is unique on at least one side (`(patient_id, encounter_date)` instead of `patient_id` alone), or aggregate before joining (one row per patient per drug, after deduplication).

## When to use which join, in practice

| Scenario | Join type |
|---|---|
| "Patients with labs *and* demographics" | inner |
| "All patients in my cohort, with labs where available" | left, with demographics as the left table |
| "All patients who appear anywhere" (data reconciliation) | full outer |
| "Patients in my cohort with *no* labs" | anti-join (`~isin`) |
| "All meds for all patients in my cohort, even patients with no meds" | left, with patient_id as the left table |
| "Mean CRP for patients on biologic therapy" | inner of (patients + biologic prescription + labs); also compute the anti-join to see who you dropped at each step |
| "Cohort attrition report" | anti-join at every step, summarized as a table of (step, patients_lost, characteristics_of_lost) |

The decision is always: which patients should be in the result, which should not, and why? The join type encodes the answer. State the answer in plain English before writing the join.
