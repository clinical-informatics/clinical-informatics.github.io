## What an algorithm is

An algorithm is a finite list of named operations on named inputs. That is the whole definition. Calling something an algorithm does not make it correct, optimal, or trustworthy. It means the procedure has been specified clearly enough that another person, or a computer, could follow it without further interpretation.

Every clinical decision rule and clinical score is an algorithm. CHA₂DS₂-VASc, Wells, Centor, qSOFA, MELD, APGAR, GCS, DAS28: each is a finite set of operations on a small number of inputs. You have run hundreds of them in your training. Reading one as an algorithm (instead of running it) is the move of this track.

## The shape of most clinical scores

Most published clinical scores share a common skeleton:

1. **Inputs.** A small set of patient-level data points. Usually four to twelve.
2. **Transformations.** A function applied to each input that converts the raw value into a contribution. Common transformations:
   - **Identity.** The input goes in as-is, sometimes with a binary "above threshold" recoding. (CHA₂DS₂-VASc does this: each criterion either contributes its weight or doesn't.)
   - **Square root.** Compresses large values relative to small ones. The difference between 4 and 9 (square roots 2 and 3) is larger than the difference between 16 and 25 (square roots 4 and 5). Used when the clinical impact of an input shows diminishing returns at high values.
   - **Logarithm.** Compresses very large values even more aggressively than square root. The difference between log(2) and log(10) is larger than the difference between log(100) and log(1000). Used for biomarkers that span orders of magnitude (CRP, ferritin, troponin, BNP, viral load).
   - **Indicator.** A binary version of an input ("present" or "absent"). Used when only the existence of a finding matters, not its magnitude.
3. **Weights.** Each transformed input gets multiplied by a coefficient. The coefficient is what tells you how much that input contributes to the final score relative to the others. Coefficients usually come from fitting a regression model on the development cohort.
4. **A constant (intercept).** A baseline value added regardless of inputs. Usually small. Sometimes negative.
5. **A sum.** The transformed-and-weighted contributions plus the constant make the raw score.
6. **Cutoffs.** Separate from the formula: thresholds that bin the continuous score into clinical decision categories. Cutoffs are clinical conventions, often arrived at through expert consensus or by maximizing some statistical objective on the development cohort. They are not part of the math; they are added on top of it.

DAS28 fits this skeleton exactly. The notebook walks each line.

## Reading versus running

Reading an algorithm asks different questions than running it. Running asks: what is the score for this patient? Reading asks four questions about each line of the formula:

1. **What is this transformation doing?** Square root? Log? Identity? Indicator? Each shape has a clinical reading: diminishing returns, order-of-magnitude compression, threshold logic.
2. **Why is the weight the size it is?** What does it imply about the relative importance of this input? What would change if it were doubled? Halved?
3. **What happens at the edges?** What does the line do when its input is zero, missing, or at maximum?
4. **Where does this weight come from?** Was it fit on a particular population? A particular era? A particular outcome definition? The same formula can fit a different patient population poorly.

If you can answer all four for every line of a score, you understand the algorithm. If you cannot, you are running it on faith.

## What DAS28 is

DAS28 is the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints. The "28" refers to a count of joints assessed for tenderness and for swelling: the proximal interphalangeal joints, metacarpophalangeal joints, wrists, elbows, shoulders, and knees, bilaterally. It is the standard composite measure of rheumatoid arthritis activity and is used both in clinical practice and in trials.

The score has two common versions: DAS28-CRP (using C-reactive protein as the acute-phase reactant) and DAS28-ESR (using the erythrocyte sedimentation rate). The notebook walks the CRP version. The two versions use slightly different coefficients and slightly different cutoffs. The cutoffs themselves have a small revision literature. The original EULAR thresholds (less than 2.6 remission, up to 3.2 low, up to 5.1 moderate, above 5.1 high) are the ones most rheumatologists carry in their head, so they are the ones the notebook uses. Which cutoff to trust is exactly the kind of "where does this number come from" interrogation Track 05 will teach you to do reflexively.

## The four inputs

- **TJC28 (tender joint count, twenty-eight joints).** Integer, zero to twenty-eight. The number of joints from the assessed twenty-eight that are tender on examination.
- **SJC28 (swollen joint count, twenty-eight joints).** Integer, zero to twenty-eight. The number of those joints that are swollen on examination.
- **CRP (C-reactive protein).** Continuous, milligrams per liter. The acute-phase reactant.
- **PGA (patient global assessment).** Continuous, zero to one hundred millimeters on a visual analog scale. The patient's own rating of overall disease activity.

Four inputs. Track 03's abstraction discipline is on full display: a measure for assessing a chronic systemic disease has chosen to ignore every other available data point about the patient (other joint counts, every other lab, medication history, prior flare frequency, imaging) and still produces a clinically useful score.
