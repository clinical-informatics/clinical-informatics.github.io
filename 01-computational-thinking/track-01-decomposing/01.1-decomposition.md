## What it is

Decomposition is the act of taking a problem stated as one big thing and breaking it into smaller named things you can examine one at a time.

Clinicians do this constantly. When a patient says they "haven't been feeling well," the history-taking decomposes the complaint: pain or not, where, character, onset, evolution, modifying factors. Each piece has its own answer. The decomposition is what allows the encounter to go anywhere.

When an alert misbehaves, the same move works. "The sepsis alert is broken" is a problem nobody can fix. "The cohort definition includes post-op patients in their first forty-eight hours, who reliably present with sympathetic arousal and atelectatic fevers, and the rule fires on them" is a problem someone can fix.

The move is the same. Only the object is different.

## Why it matters in informatics

Most clinical decision rules, scores, and models arrive as a single black box. *"The deterioration index predicts which patients will deteriorate in the next twenty-four hours."* Fine, but how?

Without names for the parts, no critique is possible, no change is specifiable, no failure mode is locatable. The only available response is reactive: "we tried it and people didn't like it." With names for the parts, the response is specific: "the cohort excludes pediatric ICU, which is where most of our deterioration events occur. We need to expand it." That is a conversation that can lead somewhere. The same applies in reading a paper, evaluating a vendor, or sitting in a CDS oversight meeting. Decomposition supplies the vocabulary to disagree productively.

## The six parts of any clinical decision rule

Most clinical rules and alerts decompose into a small set of named pieces. The notebook walks them on a worked sepsis example. Here they are in writing:

1. **The cohort.** Which patients is the rule running on? Inpatients only? ICU only? Adults? Patients with a specific order? Most "the rule fires on patients it shouldn't" problems are cohort problems.
2. **The signals.** Which data points does the rule check? Heart rate, temperature, lactate, MAP, a particular ICD-10 code, an order pattern, a vendor risk score? Most "the rule misses patients it should catch" problems are signal problems.
3. **The thresholds.** What value of each signal counts? HR > 100? Lactate > 2? Two of three SIRS criteria? Thresholds give you the most direct trade between sensitivity and specificity. Moving a threshold doesn't fix a rule that's looking at the wrong things, but it lets you tune one that's looking at the right ones.
4. **The time window.** Over what period do the signals have to be satisfied? Most recent value? Any value in the last four hours? Sustained criteria across two consecutive readings? A too-short window misses slow trends. A too-long window keeps firing on patients who have long since stabilized.
5. **The data source.** Where does each signal come from? The flowsheet, the lab system, the order system, a vendor score? Different sources have different latencies, different completeness, and different definitions for the "same" measurement. Many "the rule says one thing but the chart says another" problems are data source problems.
6. **The trigger moment.** When does the rule actually fire? Continuously as data arrives? Every fifteen minutes? When a clinician opens the chart? At order entry? A rule that fires at the wrong moment in the workflow gets ignored even when it's right.

Some rules are smaller than this. A Wells score for PE is cohort plus signals plus thresholds and nothing else. Some are bigger. Six is a working list that covers most clinical rules in practice.

## What decomposition is not

- Not enumeration. Listing every possible feature of a problem is busywork. Naming the load-bearing parts is the move.
- Not reductionism. Some problems live in the *interactions* between parts. Naming the parts is how you reach those interactions, not how you avoid them.
- Not permanent. The right decomposition of "why is this alert broken" will look different in a year, after the population has shifted, the medications have changed, and the workflow has been redesigned. Decompose again.

## What to do with this

When you meet any clinical decision rule, ask the six questions out loud. If you can answer all six, you understand the rule. If you cannot, you have a list of things to find out.

In a meeting about a misbehaving rule, the same six questions keep the conversation honest. Most arguments about CDS stall because nobody has named which part is broken. As soon as someone does, the conversation can move.

The notebook in this track applies the six parts to a sepsis alert that fires forty times a day. Track 02 takes the rule you end up with and pulls on it the other way: adding conditions, generating edge cases, watching how each new condition trades sensitivity for specificity. Together the two tracks cover the basic critical-reading move for any rule, score, or model.
