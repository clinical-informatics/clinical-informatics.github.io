## What a pattern is

A clinical decision rule is a pattern. *"Adult inpatients meeting two SIRS criteria with chart evidence of an infectious source are likely septic."* That sentence does an enormous amount of work. It abstracts over thousands of patients with broadly the same phenotype and reduces the question to a small set of checks. Rules are useful because that abstraction holds for most patients in the target population.

The question is which patients are in that "most" and which patients are not.

## What an edge case is

An edge case is a specific situation where a working rule produces a wrong answer. Two kinds:

- **False positive.** The rule fires; the patient is not the case the rule is for. King, POD 1, sympathetic arousal plus atelectatic fever. SIRS criteria met for entirely non-infectious reasons.
- **False negative.** The rule should fire; it does not. Boone, 88, urosepsis presenting as delirium, on metoprolol that has flattened his chronotropic response. He is the case the rule was designed for. His presentation does not conform to the expected phenotype.

Both kinds are predictable in advance from the rule's pattern. They come from a small set of recurring sources. Knowing the list is most of what makes a clinician useful in a CDS design meeting:

1. **Phenotypic mimics.** Distinct processes producing the same surface signs. Sedative-hypnotic withdrawal, perioperative SIRS, drug fever, atelectasis, FNHTR, postprandial thermogenesis. All of these can produce SIRS-positive vitals without infection.
2. **Suppressed signals.** Pharmacology or chronic physiology muting the signal the rule depends on. Beta-blockade blunting heart rate. Scheduled antipyretics masking fever. Cirrhotic baseline hypothermia. Chronic corticosteroids attenuating the febrile response. Patients with autonomic neuropathy who can't tachycardia even when bacteremic.
3. **Off-cohort patients.** The rule was validated on a target population and is now being applied outside it. The classic version is a medicine-cohort rule running on every inpatient in the hospital.
4. **Off-window data.** The signal is real but lives in the wrong time period. The patient was febrile twenty-four hours ago and has been afebrile since. The rule sees the older reading.
5. **Missing data.** The rule cannot see what isn't recorded. If no lactate has been drawn, the rule has no opinion. The absence itself is frequently informative ("no one thought to draw it") but the rule treats absence as silence.

## Generating edge cases on purpose

The skill of this track is producing those cases deliberately, before the rule goes live, rather than waiting for clinicians to discover them in production. A working method:

1. State the rule in one plain-English sentence.
2. Name the canonical positive case. The kind of patient the rule was designed for.
3. List three patients who match the rule's surface phenotype but are not the case the rule is for. Use the five sources above as prompts: mimics, suppressed signals, off-cohort, off-window, missing data.
4. List three patients who are the case but do not match the surface phenotype. Same prompts.
5. For each, write one sentence on what specifically about the patient breaks the pattern.

The list at the end is your catalogue. The rule is a working specification plus a known catalogue. The catalogue is what tells you what to add to nursing-education materials and what to monitor for in post-deployment surveillance.

## The trade

Adding a condition to a rule never only improves it. Every condition is a trade:

- **Tightening** (an AND of an additional condition) reduces false positives at the cost of new false negatives. Requiring chart evidence of an infectious source removes the post-op pain crowd. It also misses the newly admitted patient with occult sepsis whose workup is still in flight.
- **Loosening** (an OR of an alternative condition) reduces false negatives at the cost of new false positives. Adding "or, if lactate > 2, accept a milder tachycardia" catches the beta-blocked patient. It also catches the post-ictal patient whose type B lactic acidosis has nothing to do with infection.
- **Replacing** one condition with another swaps one edge case set for a different one. Using shock index in place of an absolute HR threshold catches the chronotropically blunted patient and misses the young patient who compensates with tachycardia until they are nearly arrested.

Conditions cannot be added in a way that only improves the rule. The numbers move in both directions every time. The job is to choose the trades knowingly, document the edge cases you have accepted, and monitor for the ones you did not anticipate.

## What a mature rule looks like

A mature clinical decision rule has four parts on the page:

1. **The rule itself**, in plain English.
2. **The canonical positive case.** A worked clinical vignette.
3. **The known false positives**, each annotated with the named confounder.
4. **The known false negatives**, each annotated with the named reason the rule can't see them.

Most CDS papers report only the first. The other three are what tells you whether to trust the rule on your floor, with your population, with your medication list, with your typical post-op throughput.
