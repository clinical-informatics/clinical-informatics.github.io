## The framework

The first four tracks covered the moves of computational thinking. This track covers the question that comes after: when, given a working algorithm, to act on its output.

The four-question framework:

1. **What was it trained on?**
2. **What does it optimize for?**
3. **Where does it fail?**
4. **Who does it fail for?**

Answer all four for any clinical algorithm in front of you, and you can decide whether to use it. If you cannot, you are deciding by trust, and trust is a separate decision that should be made with your eyes open.

## Question 1: What was it trained on?

A machine-learned model is a memory of the patients it saw during training. Knowing which patients those were is most of what you need to know about the model.

The specific things to ask:

- Which institutions? Academic centers, community hospitals, safety-net systems, integrated networks? Each has a different case mix, different staffing thresholds, different documentation practices.
- Which era? Pre-pandemic, post-pandemic, post-EHR-upgrade, post-EMTALA-policy-change?
- Which inclusion and exclusion criteria? Adults only? Specific service lines? Specific encounter types? Patients with at least one prior admission?
- Which outcome definition? "Deterioration" is the most slippery word in this literature. Trace exactly how it was defined: ICU transfer, rapid response activation, vasopressor initiation, intubation, death? At what time horizon? Adjudicated how?
- How was it sampled? Consecutive admissions or filtered down somehow? Snapshot at one time per encounter or longitudinal samples? Retrospective chart abstraction or prospective data capture?
- What was the prevalence of the outcome in training?

The answer to *what was it trained on* is a constraint on where the model is expected to work. A score trained on consecutive academic-medical-center admissions between 2017 and 2022, with the outcome defined as rapid response activation, has no claim on a community hospital without a rapid response team in 2026. The model has no memory of patients like the ones you are about to ask it to predict.

## Question 2: What does it optimize for?

Every model is tuned against a loss function, and every alert is gated by a threshold. Knowing both is required to know what the score is doing.

What to ask:

- What is the prediction horizon? The next four hours? Twenty-four? End of admission?
- Where is the alert threshold set? By whom? Vendor default, hospital-tuned, individual user-tuned?
- What is the implicit cost ratio between false positives and false negatives that produced the threshold? A score tuned for 1:1 fires far more often than one tuned for 10:1.
- What is the actionable next step the score implies? The model fires; what is the clinician supposed to *do*?

A model whose prediction horizon does not align with the actionable interval in your workflow is not useful for your workflow, regardless of its AUC. A model whose threshold is tuned to a different cost ratio than the one your floor actually faces will produce too many or too few alerts at your site. Both are Q2 failures, and neither shows up in the AUC the vendor leads with.

## Question 3: Where does it fail?

Models fail predictably. The recurring sources are mostly the ones from Track 02 applied to deployment instead of rule design:

- **Distribution shift.** The deployment population differs from the development population on something the model is using. Older patients. Different baseline vitals. Different lab-assay calibrations across EHR vendors. Different proportions of complex chronic disease.
- **Concept drift.** The relationship between features and outcomes has changed since training. Sepsis screening pre- and post-COVID is the canonical recent example.
- **Data quality shifts.** An EHR upgrade reshapes a feature. The model is still expecting the old shape and silently produces lower-quality predictions.
- **Out-of-distribution patients.** Patients who do not resemble anyone in the training set. The model produces a confident-looking number anyway, because most modern models do not refuse.
- **Workflow-induced bias.** The development team measured the model in a setting where the prediction itself did not change clinical behavior. The deployed model changes clinical behavior, which means the data it sees in deployment is different from the data it was trained on.

What to ask the vendor: how was the model tested across institutions and across time periods? What is the documented monitoring strategy for drift? What happens when key inputs are missing?

## Question 4: Who does it fail for?

This is the question most often skipped and the one that produces most of the harms that show up after deployment.

What to ask:

- What was the subgroup composition of the training data? By race, age, sex, primary language, insurance class, encounter type?
- What is the subgroup performance, broken out? AUC, calibration, alert rate, alert positive predictive value, all by subgroup?
- Has the model been validated specifically in subgroups that match *your* catchment, not a generic "diverse" cohort?
- Are there subgroups where performance has not been measured?

A model that has not been validated in a subgroup that constitutes a meaningful fraction of your catchment is, for that subgroup, an experiment. You are allowed to run an experiment, but you have to call it what it is, with the consent, monitoring, and recourse structures that come with experiments.

The Obermeyer and Vyas literature already cited in Track 03 lives in this question. The model designers had answers to Q1 and Q2 and a strong Q3 story. They had no useful Q4 answer, and that was where the harm was.

## Putting them together

The four questions do not have to point at the same gap. Often a model is fine on Q1 (training cohort resembles yours) but fails on Q4 (subgroup performance unmeasured for the segment that is a third of your patients). Or it is fine on Q4 but fails on Q2 (the prediction horizon does not match anything your workflow can act on). Identifying which of the four questions is the gap tells you what to ask the vendor next and what to do at your site.

The deployment patterns when the answers reveal a gap:

- **All four green.** Deploy as designed.
- **Q1 questionable.** Recalibrate on local data before deployment. The model is the right shape; the population in its memory is wrong.
- **Q2 questionable.** Re-tune the threshold for your cost ratio and workflow. The math is right; the dial is not.
- **Q3 questionable.** Pilot before full deployment. Monitor for the failure modes the team named.
- **Q4 questionable.** Subgroup-specific validation before deployment, with a subgroup performance monitoring plan in writing.
- **Multiple gaps.** Decline, or pilot under explicit experimental governance. The score is not ready for your hospital with what you currently know, no matter what its AUC is.

## What "FDA-cleared" does not answer

FDA clearance for a clinical decision support tool is necessary for some uses and sufficient for none. The four questions still apply. Clearance means the FDA evaluated the model. It does not mean the model was evaluated *for your hospital, your case mix, your subgroup composition.* Treat clearance as a precondition for some deployments, not a substitute for any of the four questions. The same applies to "validated across N institutions" and "AUC 0.8X." Those are cover slides, not answers.
