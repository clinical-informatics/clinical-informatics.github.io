## Go deeper: Writing about data clearly

**If you want the federal-government guidance on plain language:**

- [The Plain Language guide series at digital.gov](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language) is the U.S. General Services Administration's working guidance for federal communicators, descended from the Plain Writing Act of 2010. The series defines plain language operationally (short sentences, active voice, common words, one idea per sentence) and covers audience analysis, document structure, and revision. The guidance is written for federal-government writers but applies directly to clinical-informatics writing, which has the same characteristic challenge: communicating regulated and technical content to non-specialist readers.

**If you want the UX-research backing for the same recommendations:**

- [Nielsen Norman Group's "Plain Language Is for Everyone, Even Experts"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/plain-language-experts/) is the user-experience research argument for plain language. NNG's empirical work on how professional readers (lawyers, executives, clinicians) actually read on screens has consistently found that plain-language versions of technical documents are read faster, understood better, and remembered longer by the same expert readers who would describe themselves as preferring formal prose. The article is the citation when an executive argues that "we need to sound professional."

**If you want to go significantly further into the data-writing craft:**

- [Stephen Few's Perceptual Edge](https://www.perceptualedge.com/) is the public archive (2003 to 2017) of Stephen Few's essays on data communication. Few's work is the most substantive long-form treatment of clear data writing produced by any single author in the past two decades. The essays specifically on the language of data communication (what to say in a chart caption, how to write a finding, how to distinguish description from inference) are the deep reading.
