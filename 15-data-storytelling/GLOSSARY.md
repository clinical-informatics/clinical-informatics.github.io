# Glossary: 15 Data storytelling

The communication-craft vocabulary specific to this course appears below. The shared terms (HIPAA, FHIR, OMOP, USCDI, the analytic vocabulary) are defined in the [curriculum-wide glossary](../start-here/GLOSSARY.md).

**Audience analysis.** The deliberate practice of identifying who will read or hear a communication and what they need from it before deciding what to say. Audience analysis is the prerequisite move for every communication choice that follows: the message, the length, the level of detail, the visual choices, the order of presentation. In clinical informatics, the four operationally distinct audiences are technical (engineers, data scientists, analysts), clinical (clinicians, nurses, pharmacists), executive (CMIO, CMO, CIO, CFO), and patient (patients, family members, and patient-and-family advisory councils).

**The right altitude.** The level of detail at which the message lives. Technical audiences want low altitude (the data definitions, the algorithm, the exact API calls). Executive audiences want high altitude (the KPI move, the dollar number, the risk). Most failed communications fly at the wrong altitude for the audience. The skill is choosing the altitude before choosing the content, and holding to it throughout.

**Plain English.** Writing that is short, direct, active, and free of jargon, with one idea per sentence and short paragraphs. Plain English is the default register for any audience that is not specifically technical, and it is the register most clinical staff prefer even when they are technically capable readers. Plain English is not the same as oversimplified; it is the same precision written in language a non-specialist reader can act on.

**BLUF (Bottom Line Up Front).** An executive-communication structure in which the conclusion or recommendation appears in the first sentence, before the supporting context, evidence, and reasoning. BLUF inverts the academic-paper structure (which builds toward a conclusion); the inversion matches how an executive reader actually reads. The reader can stop after one sentence and still have the substantively important content.

**Finding, Implication, Recommendation (FIR).** A three-part narrative structure that turns a data observation into an actionable recommendation. The finding is what the data shows (specific, sourced, quantified). The implication is what the finding means for the listener's domain (clinical practice, financial position, regulatory posture). The recommendation is what action the listener should take (specific, time-bound, owned). Each part is necessary; weak storytelling typically omits the implication and jumps from finding to recommendation, which leaves the listener unable to verify the logic.

**Lead with the claim.** The recommendation to state the substantive conclusion first and then back it up with evidence and reasoning, rather than building toward the conclusion. The inverse practice (build, then conclude) is common in academic writing and is generally counterproductive in clinical-informatics communication: the audience is reading to make a decision, not to follow the writer's thinking.

**Hedging.** Language that softens a claim with words like "may," "appears to," "suggests," "consistent with." Hedging that conveys real uncertainty is honest; hedging that conveys epistemic posturing or self-protection is a distraction. The communication-craft move is to convey the same statistical uncertainty in language an audience can act on, which usually means stating the central estimate plainly and quantifying the range separately.

**Headline number.** The single number that most efficiently conveys the substantive finding. The headline number is usually an absolute reduction, a relative reduction, a dollar number, or a count, depending on what the audience can act on. Choosing the right headline is the most important sentence-level craft decision in writing about data: the wrong headline buries a real finding under a less actionable framing.

**Translation.** Rendering the same fact for different audiences without distorting it. Good translation preserves the substantive claim and the underlying uncertainty while changing the vocabulary, the altitude, and the level of detail. Translation is the operational form of audience analysis.

**Executive summary.** A short (one or two paragraphs, occasionally one page) summary of a longer document, written for a reader who may not read the longer document. A good executive summary stands alone: the recipient can read only the executive summary and make the decision the longer document supports.

**One-pager.** A single-page deliverable that an executive can read in a meeting or between meetings. The one-pager is the standard clinical-informatics communication artifact for executive review: a problem statement, a finding, a recommendation, an ask, and a single illustrative chart, all on one page. Multi-page documents are read by exception.

**Annotation.** Text or graphical overlay on a chart that helps interpretation. Common annotations include the reference range, the intervention timepoint, the period of interest, and the value at a specific point in time. A chart for a clinical audience usually needs more annotation than a chart for a technical audience; a chart for an executive audience usually needs the most. Good annotation answers a question the chart raises; bad annotation repeats what the chart already shows.

**Chart as evidence vs chart as rhetoric.** Two distinct purposes for the same chart. Chart-as-evidence presents the data and lets the reader draw the conclusion (the academic-paper figure). Chart-as-rhetoric presents the data in service of a specific argument (the executive presentation figure). Both are legitimate; the communication-craft skill is to know which you are producing and to be honest about it.

**The clinical story.** The human narrative that gives data its meaning. Ms. Reyes's CRP series across four years is data; the story is the patient with seropositive RA whose disease activity rose, who switched to a biologic, and whose inflammation came under control. The story is what makes the data memorable and actionable; the data alone is rarely persuasive on its own.

**Vendor evaluation question.** A question designed to surface what a vendor would prefer the buyer not see. Good vendor questions are specific (asking about a precise mechanism rather than the marketing summary), comparative (asking how a feature compares to an explicit alternative), and operational (asking about what happens in failure modes). The communication-craft skill is asking the question that produces the most informative answer, not the question the vendor has practiced answering.

**The clinician as domain expert.** The principle that the clinician's role in a vendor or AI-team meeting is to know what good clinical work looks like, not what good machine learning looks like. The vendor knows machine learning; the clinician knows when an AI-generated recommendation makes clinical sense and when it does not. The clinician's questions should come from that authority, not from a position of technical deference.

**Throat-clearing.** Conversational filler that delays the substantive content of a sentence or paragraph: "It is important to note that," "What I want to say is," "One thing worth pointing out." Throat-clearing wastes the reader's attention in the early sentences when attention is highest. The craft move is to delete the throat-clearing and lead with the substantive claim.

**Specificity.** The quality of being concrete rather than general. "The new alert reduced 30-day mortality from 5.7% to 4.5%" is specific; "the new alert improves patient outcomes" is general. Specificity is one of the most reliable craft moves in writing about data: it converts a forgettable abstraction into a memorable claim.

**The ask.** The action the speaker wants the audience to take. Every executive presentation or memo has an ask, whether or not it is named. Naming the ask explicitly ("approve the $480K capital expenditure for the sepsis CDS rollout") is the strongest predictor of whether the action follows the communication. Communications without an explicit ask are exploratory at best.
