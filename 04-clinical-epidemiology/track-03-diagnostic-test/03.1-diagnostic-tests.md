# 03.1 Diagnostic test performance

A diagnostic test takes a measurement (an antibody titer, a finding on imaging, the score from a clinical decision rule) and returns one of two answers: above the cutoff or below. The clinical decision the test is supposed to inform is also binary: act as if the patient has the condition, or do not. Most of the conceptual confusion in this area comes from forgetting that the test result and the underlying disease state are two different binary variables, not one.

## 1. The 2x2 table

Put the underlying disease state on one axis (present vs absent, by some reference standard) and the test result on the other (positive vs negative). Every patient lands in exactly one of the four cells:

|                         | Disease present       | Disease absent        |
|-------------------------|-----------------------|-----------------------|
| **Test positive**       | True positive (TP)    | False positive (FP)   |
| **Test negative**       | False negative (FN)   | True negative (TN)    |

The disease-state column totals and the test-result row totals are both important, and they answer different clinical questions.

## 2. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV

These four measures are just the four conditional probabilities you can compute from the 2x2 table. The arithmetic is trivial; what matters is which probability you actually wanted.

- **Sensitivity** = TP / (TP + FN). The fraction of patients *with* the disease who test positive. Read the disease-present column. A property of the test in patients who have the condition.
- **Specificity** = TN / (TN + FP). The fraction of patients *without* the disease who test negative. Read the disease-absent column. A property of the test in patients who do not have the condition.
- **Positive predictive value (PPV)** = TP / (TP + FP). The fraction of *positive tests* that come from patients who actually have the disease. Read the test-positive row.
- **Negative predictive value (NPV)** = TN / (TN + FN). The fraction of *negative tests* that come from patients who actually do not have the disease. Read the test-negative row.

Two distinctions matter:

1. **Sens and spec are properties of the test.** They depend on the cutoff and the measurement technology, but they do not depend on how common the disease is in the population the test is being used on. They are stable across clinics.
2. **PPV and NPV are properties of the test *in the population it is being used on*.** They depend on prevalence as much as on the test itself. PPV and NPV are what a clinician actually wants to know (the patient just tested positive; what is the probability they have the disease?). Sens and spec are what a manufacturer publishes.

## 3. The Bayesian collapse

The single most-missed point in clinical diagnostics: PPV depends on prevalence, and at low prevalence PPV collapses *even for very good tests*. The arithmetic forces this. Imagine a population of 10,000 people and a test with sens 95%, spec 95%.

- At a prevalence of 30% (3,000 with disease, 7,000 without), the test produces 2,850 true positives and 350 false positives. PPV = 2,850 / 3,200 ≈ 89%.
- At a prevalence of 1% (100 with disease, 9,900 without), the same test produces 95 true positives and 495 false positives. PPV = 95 / 590 ≈ 16%.

The test got no worse. The denominators changed. At low prevalence, the false-positive arithmetic dominates because there are so many more disease-free people in the population than diseased people.

This is the Bayesian intuition exercise. It is the reason population screening of asymptomatic people for rare diseases generates large numbers of false positives even with sensitive and specific tests, and it is the reason a positive result in a high-pretest-probability patient is far more reassuring than a positive result in a low-pretest-probability one. The clinical move that follows: ask "what is the prevalence in *my* clinical setting?" before you interpret a test result.

## 4. Likelihood ratios

Likelihood ratios collapse the four measures down to two numbers that move pre-test probability to post-test probability. They are the clinician-facing form of Bayes' rule.

- **LR+** = sens / (1 - spec). How much a positive test increases the odds of disease.
- **LR-** = (1 - sens) / spec. How much a negative test decreases the odds of disease.

The operative arithmetic is:

> post-test odds = pre-test odds × LR

Odds and probability are interchangeable (odds = p / (1 - p)). The reason LRs work the way they do is that Bayes' rule, applied to the diagnostic-test setup, takes exactly this form.

McGee's 2002 simplification is the bedside-usable version: an LR+ of 2, 5, or 10 increases post-test probability by about 15, 30, or 45 percentage points; an LR- of 0.5, 0.2, or 0.1 decreases it by the same amounts. Round numbers, no calculator, no nomogram. The mental model: a "good" LR+ is at least 5, a "good" LR- is at most 0.2, and anything closer to 1.0 is barely moving the needle.

LRs are the way to compare two tests at a glance. A test with sens 95% / spec 50% has LR+ of 1.9, which barely changes anything; a test with sens 50% / spec 95% has LR+ of 10, which moves a 30% pre-test probability to about 80%. The two tests have the same average sens+spec but very different clinical action.

## 5. ROC curves and AUC

Most clinical "tests" are not really binary. They produce a continuous number (anti-CCP titer, troponin level, a risk score), and someone has picked a cutoff to turn the number into a yes/no. The cutoff is a *choice*, and changing it trades sensitivity for specificity.

The **receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve** plots that trade-off. The x-axis is 1 - specificity (the false-positive rate). The y-axis is sensitivity (the true-positive rate). Each point on the curve corresponds to one possible cutoff. The curve always passes through (0, 0) (when the cutoff is so high that no one tests positive, sens = 0 and false-positive rate = 0) and (1, 1) (when the cutoff is so low that everyone tests positive, sens = 1 and false-positive rate = 1).

The shape of the curve tells you how well the test separates diseased from non-diseased patients. A test with perfect separation has a curve that goes straight up to (0, 1) and then across to (1, 1); a test with no separation lies on the diagonal.

The **area under the ROC curve (AUC)** summarizes the shape into one number. The geometric interpretation: AUC is the probability that a randomly chosen diseased patient has a higher test value than a randomly chosen non-diseased patient. An AUC of 0.5 means the test is no better than a coin flip; an AUC of 1.0 means the test perfectly orders diseased above non-diseased. Anti-CCP in published meta-analyses has an AUC around 0.94.

AUC is a useful single number for comparing tests, but it is not a substitute for picking the right cutoff. A test with high AUC can still be useless at the cutoff that actually fits the clinical workflow. The "operating point" question (which cutoff to use, given the clinical cost of false positives and false negatives) is a separate decision from the discrimination question (how well does the test rank patients), and Track 04 of Course 11 (decision curve analysis) returns to it.

A second limitation: AUC measures *discrimination*, the ability to order patients correctly. It says nothing about *calibration*, the accuracy of the probability the test or model assigns. A well-discriminating test can still be badly calibrated, and Course 09 (AI in medicine) takes that distinction apart.

## 6. The reflex this track is trying to install

When you order a test, or read a test result in a paper, ask:

1. **What is the pre-test probability in this setting?** Without this number, sens and spec cannot be turned into PPV and NPV.
2. **What is the LR+ and LR- of this test at this cutoff?** A test with LRs near 1 is decorative; a test with LRs far from 1 changes the answer.
3. **What ROC operating point did the published study pick, and is it the one I want clinically?** The "optimal" cutoff that maximizes sens + spec is not always the one you want at the bedside.

These three questions catch the recurring failure modes: applying a high-prevalence test in a low-prevalence setting and over-believing positives, ordering a test with poor LRs and acting on it anyway, and adopting a cutoff that maximizes one metric while ignoring the clinical cost of the failure mode that metric does not catch.
