# 02.1 Bias: the number you see can be the wrong number

A bias is any systematic reason the number coming out of a study is not the number you would have gotten in the world. Bias is *not* the same as imprecision. A small study with wide confidence intervals is imprecise; a large study that systematically over-samples healthier patients is biased. More data does not fix bias; it just gives you a tighter estimate of the wrong number.

Bias has three classical families: **selection bias**, **information bias**, and **confounding**. They have different mechanisms, they need different fixes, and they often co-exist. The point of this track is not to memorize the taxonomy but to give you a reflex: when you read a result, the first question to ask is "which way could this be off, and why?"

## 1. Selection bias

Selection bias occurs when the *way patients enter or leave the study* depends on both the exposure and the outcome. It comes in several specific flavors:

- **Incidence-prevalence bias** (Neyman bias). You sample *current cases* of a disease for a cross-sectional study, but the patients who survive long enough to be in your sampling frame are systematically different from the patients who died early. Studies of "current heart failure patients" miss the patients who died of cardiogenic shock in week one, so risk factors that drive early mortality look smaller than they are.
- **Healthy-user bias** (and its sibling, healthy-adherer bias). The patients who choose a new therapy, or who actually take the therapy they were prescribed, tend to also do other healthy things (diet, exercise, follow-up visits). Observed benefits of the therapy include the benefit of being the kind of person who shows up. The classic example is the apparent benefit of postmenopausal hormone therapy in observational studies, which largely vanished when WHI randomized it.
- **Loss to follow-up.** Patients who leave the study look different from patients who stay. If sicker patients drop out of a trial of an intervention, the intervention arm looks better than it is.
- **Berkson's bias** (admission bias). When you study disease associations in hospitalized patients, the association is distorted by the fact that having two conditions makes you more likely to be admitted than having one. We come back to this under "collider" below, because Berkson's bias *is* the collider phenomenon applied to hospital sampling.
- **Survivor bias.** Studying patients who are alive at the moment of measurement is studying a survival-filtered subset. A registry of long-term cancer survivors will not look anything like the underlying population of cancer patients.

The structural feature in every case is the same: *the probability of being in the dataset depends on both the exposure and the outcome.* When that is true, the association you see in the dataset does not generalize to the population.

## 2. Information bias

Information bias occurs when the *measurement* of exposure, outcome, or covariate is systematically wrong, and the wrongness is not random.

- **Recall bias.** Patients with the outcome remember exposures more thoroughly than patients without it. Mothers of children with congenital anomalies recall medication use in pregnancy more completely than mothers of healthy children. Case-control studies of pregnancy exposures are especially vulnerable to this.
- **Misclassification.** A test, a diagnostic code, or a chart-abstraction process labels patients incorrectly. *Non-differential* misclassification (errors are equally likely in exposed and unexposed) generally pulls estimates toward the null. *Differential* misclassification (errors correlate with exposure or outcome) can push estimates in any direction, including past the truth.
- **Surveillance bias** (detection bias). Exposed patients are followed more closely than unexposed patients, so events that would have gone unnoticed in the unexposed group get caught in the exposed group. Patients on a new immunosuppressant get more lab draws than patients on a stable regimen; the "increased infection rate" includes both true biological excess and increased detection.
- **Ascertainment bias.** Different definitions of the outcome are applied to different subgroups. The classic example is "serious infection" being adjudicated more thoroughly in the experimental arm of a trial than in the control arm.
- **Contamination.** Patients assigned to one group actually receive the exposure assigned to the other. Trial of a behavioral intervention where the control arm reads about the intervention online and adopts it: the intent-to-treat effect now understates the per-protocol effect, sometimes drastically.

Information bias is fought primarily through study design: blinded outcome adjudication, prospective ascertainment, standardized measurement, and pre-specified definitions of every variable.

## 3. Confounding

Confounding is the most common and the most consequential of the three. A **confounder** is a variable that:

1. Is a cause of the outcome (independent of the exposure).
2. Is associated with the exposure (caused by the exposure's upstream determinants, or at least correlated with it through some shared cause).
3. Is *not* on the causal pathway from exposure to outcome.

If those three things are true, the crude association between the exposure and outcome will mix the real exposure effect with whatever effect the confounder is having. The classic confounder in observational therapy comparisons is *disease severity*: sicker patients get more aggressive treatment AND have worse outcomes, so the aggressive treatment looks worse than it really is. (This is known in the pharmacoepidemiology literature as **confounding by indication**, and it is the single biggest reason why observational drug-safety signals routinely fail to replicate when the same question is asked in a trial.)

The defenses against confounding fall into two categories:

- **Design defenses.** Randomization. Restriction (study only one stratum of the confounder). Matching (case-control studies).
- **Analysis defenses.** Stratification. Regression adjustment. Propensity score methods (matching, weighting, stratification). Inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting (IPW). Standardization. Instrumental variables, when a valid instrument exists.

All of these defenses share a critical precondition: *the confounder has to be measured*. Unmeasured confounders cannot be adjusted away by any technique. Negative-control outcomes and sensitivity analyses (E-values) are tools for bounding how big an unmeasured confounder would have to be to explain the observed result, but they do not fix the problem.

## 4. DAGs: the visual grammar

Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) are the canonical tool for reasoning about which covariates are confounders, which are mediators, and which are colliders. The graph has three pieces:

- **Nodes** are variables.
- **Directed edges** (arrows) are causal effects. An arrow from A to B means A causes B. The arrow is the *causal* claim, not a statistical claim.
- **Paths** are sequences of edges. A path is open if information flows through it; it is closed if conditioning on a node along the path blocks the flow.

The "acyclic" requirement means no variable can cause itself through any chain of arrows. The "directed" requirement means edges have heads and tails: the asymmetry of cause and effect is the entire point of the diagram.

Two paths matter most:

- A **causal path** is a path that follows arrows from exposure to outcome. The total causal effect of the exposure on the outcome is the combined influence along all causal paths.
- A **backdoor path** is a non-causal path from exposure to outcome that starts with an arrow *pointing into* the exposure. Backdoor paths carry confounded association. Closing all backdoor paths (by conditioning on the right set of variables) is the formal definition of "removing confounding" in the DAG framework.

The trick that DAGs do better than the older Three-Criteria definition of a confounder is that they tell you precisely when a variable should be controlled for *and when it should not*. They handle the three roles below in one consistent vocabulary.

## 5. The three roles a covariate can play

Every variable in your dataset that is not the exposure and not the outcome plays one of three roles relative to the exposure-outcome question. Naming the role determines whether you should adjust for it.

### Confounder: common cause of exposure and outcome

```text
                    Confounder
                   /          \
                  v            v
              Exposure -----> Outcome
```

A confounder sits "above" both exposure and outcome and has arrows into both. It opens a backdoor path. **Adjust for it.** Stratification, regression, propensity score methods all work; the goal is to break the dependence of the exposure on the confounder.

In the TNFi-and-infection question, **prednisone co-use** is a confounder. Severe RA patients are more likely to be on prednisone AND more likely to be stepped up to a TNFi (so prednisone correlates with TNFi). Prednisone independently increases infection risk (so prednisone causes the outcome). The crude TNFi-infection association mixes the real biological effect of TNFi with the prednisone-driven excess risk that came along for the ride.

### Mediator: on the causal path from exposure to outcome

```text
                                     Mediator
                                    /        \
                                   v          v
              Exposure ---------> ... --------> Outcome
```

A mediator sits between exposure and outcome, with an arrow from exposure into mediator and from mediator into outcome. The mediator is *how* the exposure causes the outcome. **Do NOT adjust for it if you want the total effect.** Conditioning on the mediator blocks the very path that carries the causal effect you are trying to measure, and the adjusted estimate underestimates (or zeroes out) the exposure effect.

If TNFi causes serious infection mainly *because* it produces immunosuppression (measured, say, as a composite of vaccine response and infection markers), then immunosuppression is a mediator of the TNFi-infection question. Adjusting for immunosuppression in a regression of infection on TNFi would remove the indirect effect that goes through immunosuppression, leaving only any direct biological effect of TNFi that does not pass through immunosuppression. If all of TNFi's infection-causing action goes through immunosuppression, the adjusted estimate is close to zero, and a naive reader concludes "TNFi does not cause infections" when the truth is the opposite.

(Mediator-adjustment is a legitimate move when you specifically want to estimate the **direct** effect of an exposure that does not pass through the mediator. The mediation-analysis literature has a careful machinery for this. The point here is that the routine "adjust for everything in the dataset" reflex does the wrong thing when applied to a mediator.)

### Collider: common effect of two variables

```text
              Exposure ----.        .---- Outcome
                            \      /
                             v    v
                            Collider
```

A collider sits "below" two variables, with arrows from both pointing *into* it. It is the structural opposite of a confounder. A collider sits on a closed path between exposure and outcome. **Conditioning on a collider OPENS that closed path and induces a spurious association.** This is the most counter-intuitive result in causal inference, and it is the most common DAG error in the applied literature.

The clearest clinical version is **Berkson's bias**, the admission-rate trap. Suppose TNFi has no causal effect on serious infection in the general RA population. Suppose both TNFi use and serious infections independently increase the probability of being admitted to a rheumatology referral center. A researcher who studies the cohort of admitted patients (conditioning on the collider "admission") will find a *negative* association between TNFi and infection in their data, even though the true association is zero. The negative association is a structural artifact of how patients got into the dataset.

The same structure appears under other names. **M-bias** is the case where two unmeasured confounders create a collider in the middle of a longer DAG and conditioning on that collider opens a backdoor. **Stratification on a post-treatment variable** is collider adjustment when the post-treatment variable is influenced by both treatment and outcome (or by treatment and an unmeasured cause of outcome).

The way to spot collider risk: when you find yourself "controlling for" a variable that happens *after* the exposure, or "restricting to" a subset defined by a downstream condition (hospitalized patients, patients who completed follow-up, patients with a confirmed diagnosis), pause. Draw the DAG. If the conditioning variable is a common effect of two upstream variables, you have just opened a backdoor that was closed before.

## 6. The reflex this track is trying to install

When you read a paper or a vendor evaluation, the question to ask first is not "what statistical test did they use?" The question is:

1. **What is the exposure, what is the outcome, and what covariates did they adjust for?**
2. **For each adjusted covariate, what role does it play? Confounder, mediator, or collider?**
3. **What unmeasured covariates would still be confounders if they had been measured?** (Disease severity is the perennial answer.)

A study that adjusts for the right confounders and *only* the right confounders gives you a clean estimate. A study that adjusts for a mediator gives you a direct-effect estimate that is usually not the clinically relevant one. A study that adjusts for a collider gives you a biased estimate that looks more precise than the unadjusted one and feels reassuring even though it is wrong.

The same logic applies to clinical AI tools and decision-support models. When a vendor reports that their model is "adjusted for" a long list of variables, the question is whether those variables are confounders of the prediction-outcome relationship, or whether some of them are downstream of the outcome and therefore colliders. Adjusting for a collider in a predictive model can make the model look more accurate in the development data and fail in deployment, because the structural artifact does not generalize outside the conditioning frame.

DAGs are the tool that lets you ask the question precisely. They are not magic, and they require domain knowledge about which arrows point which way. But once you can sketch one for the question in front of you, the choice of what to adjust for stops being a matter of taste and starts being a question with a defensible answer.
