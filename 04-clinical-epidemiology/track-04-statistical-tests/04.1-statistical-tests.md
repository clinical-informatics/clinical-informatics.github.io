# 04.1 Basic statistical tests

"Which test do I run?" is the wrong starting question. Most of the confusion in clinical statistics comes from skipping the variable-type question and trying to memorize the test list. Reverse the order, and the test becomes a lookup.

## 1. Read the variables first

Three things to identify before anything else:

1. **The exposure (or grouping) variable.** Is it categorical (two arms, three arms, sex, treatment vs control) or continuous (a dose, a lab value, an age)?
2. **The outcome variable.** Continuous (a lab value, a score, a duration)? Categorical (event yes/no, response categories)? Ordinal (a stage I-IV, a Likert score)? Time-to-event (the moment something happens, with patients censored when the study ends)? Count (number of events per patient)?
3. **The group structure.** One sample (compared against a known reference)? Two independent groups? Two paired measurements on the same patients? More than two groups?

Most of clinical statistics is then a lookup table indexed by those three answers, with one extra question (parametric vs non-parametric) when the outcome is continuous. The table below is the variable-type-by-variable-type matrix that drives the interactive in the notebook.

## 2. The pairings matrix

| Exposure | Outcome | Group structure | Recommended test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Categorical | Continuous | One sample | One-sample t-test (or Wilcoxon signed-rank) |
| Categorical | Continuous | Two independent | Independent t-test (or Mann-Whitney U) |
| Categorical | Continuous | Two paired | Paired t-test (or Wilcoxon signed-rank) |
| Categorical | Continuous | More than two independent | One-way ANOVA (or Kruskal-Wallis) |
| Continuous | Continuous | (n/a) | Pearson correlation or linear regression (or Spearman correlation) |
| Categorical | Categorical | (any) | Chi-square (or Fisher's exact when expected cells are small) |
| Continuous | Categorical | (n/a) | Logistic regression |
| Categorical | Ordinal | Two independent | Mann-Whitney U (ordinal as ranks) |
| Any | Ordinal | (>2 categories) | Ordinal logistic regression |
| Categorical | Time-to-event | (any) | Log-rank test (unadjusted) or Cox regression (adjusted) |
| Continuous | Time-to-event | (n/a) | Cox regression |
| Any | Count | (any) | Poisson regression (or negative binomial when overdispersed) |

The parenthetical "or" is the parametric vs non-parametric choice, addressed below. The "any" rows are tests that do not care about exposure type because the structure of the test handles both.

## 3. Parametric vs non-parametric

Parametric tests assume a specific distributional shape (usually approximately normal) and use the *means* of the samples. Non-parametric tests make no assumption about distribution and use the *ranks* instead.

The reflex to install: **non-parametric is the safer default when you do not have strong evidence the distribution is approximately normal, and the cost of using it is usually small**. When the data really are normal, the non-parametric test loses a little power (the p-value runs slightly larger) but does not give a wrong answer. When the data are skewed, have outliers, or are small samples where you cannot tell, the parametric test can give noticeably wrong answers because the mean is sensitive to the extreme values that the rank-based test ignores.

The notebook makes this concrete: on a skewed CRP-like dataset with a single outlier, the t-test fails to detect a difference that Mann-Whitney detects clearly. On the same kind of data without the outlier, both tests agree. The lesson: the difference is in the data, not in the test, and the question to ask first is whether the data look like the t-test's assumptions hold.

When to use parametric anyway:

- Sample sizes are large enough that the Central Limit Theorem makes the sampling distribution of the mean approximately normal even if the data are not (rule of thumb: n per group above 30 is usually enough for a t-test).
- The literature reports the parametric statistic and you need direct comparison.
- The continuous variable has been transformed (often log) to approximate normality before the test.

When to insist on non-parametric:

- Small samples.
- Visible skew or outliers in the data.
- Ordinal outcomes (Likert scales, disease activity categories) where the numeric labels are not really continuous.
- The data are bounded or censored in a way the normal distribution cannot represent.

## 4. P-values and confidence intervals

The p-value answers one specific question: *if the null hypothesis were true and we repeated this experiment many times, what fraction of the time would we see a result at least this extreme?* That is a useful question, and it is also routinely misread.

The Greenland 2016 paper in `go-deeper.md` walks 25 specific misinterpretations. The four that matter most clinically:

- **A p-value of 0.04 is not "96% probability the effect is real."** It is the probability of the observed (or more extreme) data *given the null hypothesis is true*. It says nothing direct about the probability the alternative hypothesis is true.
- **A p-value above 0.05 is not "no effect."** It is "insufficient evidence to reject the null at the chosen threshold." The effect may still be real and clinically important; you may simply not have enough data to see it. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
- **A statistically significant p-value is not the same as clinically important.** A trivially small difference can be highly significant with a large enough sample. The question to ask is the size of the effect, not its p-value.
- **Two studies disagree on significance is not the same as the studies disagree on the effect.** A study with n=50 and p=0.06 and a study with n=500 and p=0.001 can have the same effect estimate. The difference is in the sample size, not in the truth they each saw.

A **confidence interval** is the same calculation as a p-value, run differently. A 95% CI gives the range of values for the effect that are consistent with the data at the conventional threshold. The CI carries information the p-value collapses:

- The *direction* of the effect (the sign of the interval).
- The *magnitude* (how big the effect could be).
- The *precision* (how wide the interval is).

A study reporting effect 0.30 with 95% CI [0.05, 0.55] has a different reading than one reporting effect 0.30 with 95% CI [-0.40, 1.00], even though the first might give p=0.02 and the second p=0.38. Both observed the same effect; the second study has less information about it. The CI tells you that directly; the p-value hides it.

The notebook makes this visible: fix the true effect at 0.30 and slide the sample size from 10 to 500. The p-value shrinks toward zero. The CI tightens toward the true effect. Both move; the CI moves with information attached.

## 5. The reflex this track is trying to install

When you read a paper or design an analysis:

1. **Read the variables first.** Name the exposure, the outcome, the group structure.
2. **Pick the test from the matrix.** It is a lookup, not a memorization problem.
3. **Decide parametric or not.** Look at the data (or the literature) for evidence the distribution behaves; default to non-parametric when you cannot tell.
4. **Read the CI, not just the p-value.** The CI carries the magnitude and the precision; the p-value carries neither directly.

A paper that reports effect size, confidence interval, and (only then) a p-value is doing the right thing. A paper that reports a p-value alone is hiding the question you actually wanted answered.
