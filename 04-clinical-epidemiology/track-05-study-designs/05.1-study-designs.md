# 05.1 Study designs

The question to ask before you read another paper is *what design could have produced this evidence, and what does that mean for what we can conclude?* The six designs in this track each carry their own claim about causation, their own information cost, and their own pitfalls. Knowing which design is in front of you is the prerequisite for reading the result.

## 1. The six designs in plain English

**Randomized controlled trial (RCT).** Investigators assign exposure (treatment vs control) at random. Because of randomization, the two arms are exchangeable in expectation on all measured and unmeasured covariates. The crude treatment-effect estimate is unbiased for the causal effect. RCTs are the cleanest evidence about causation we have, and they are also the most expensive, slowest, and narrowest. Generalizability beyond the trial population is a separate question the RCT does not answer.

**Prospective cohort.** Investigators identify a group of people based on exposure status (or some other baseline characteristic), follow them forward in time, and record outcomes as they occur. Because the exposure was measured before the outcome, the temporal sequence is clean. Confounding is the central threat: exposed and unexposed patients differ by reasons other than the exposure, and only measured confounders can be adjusted for.

**Retrospective cohort.** The same logical structure as a prospective cohort, with one practical difference: the exposure and outcome data already exist (in an EHR, a registry, a claims database) at the time the study begins. The investigator reconstructs the cohort and follows it forward through historical data. Faster and cheaper than prospective, but the data were collected for clinical or administrative purposes and may not have the variables you wanted.

**Case-control.** Investigators sample by outcome: people who developed the condition (cases) and people who did not (controls). Each is then asked about prior exposure. This is the design of choice for rare outcomes where a cohort study would need to follow too many people for too long. Case-control studies give odds ratios, not risk ratios; they cannot estimate incidence or risk directly because the case-to-control ratio is set by the investigator. They are particularly susceptible to recall bias and selection bias in the choice of controls.

**Cross-sectional.** A single point in time. Investigators measure exposure and outcome simultaneously on the same patients. This is the right design for *prevalence* questions and for *associations at a moment*. It is the wrong design for *causation* questions because the temporal sequence between exposure and outcome cannot be established. It is also the wrong design for *incidence* (no follow-up) and for any time-dependent measure (no person-time).

**Case series (or case report).** A clinical description of one or a small number of patients. Hypothesis-generating, not hypothesis-testing. Useful for novel presentations, rare diseases, or unusual outcomes. The first step of evidence, not the last.

## 2. The five-question framework

Most of the design choice is a lookup once you have answered five questions:

1. **What is the exposure?** A treatment, an environmental factor, a clinical state, a genetic variant?
2. **What is the outcome?** A diagnosis, a lab value, an event, a death, a quality-of-life score?
3. **Where is the point of observation?** Do you assign the exposure, follow patients who already have it, or look back from people who have the outcome?
4. **What is being measured?** Prevalence, incidence, association, causation, time-to-event?
5. **Is there a time component?** Are patients followed forward, or is everything captured at one moment?

The interactive in the notebook walks these five and returns the matching design plus the reasoning. The same questions cover almost every clinical paper you will read.

## 3. Longitudinal designs: person-time, incidence, and the HR

Tracks 1 and 2 introduced incidence rates and hazard ratios. Those measures need *follow-up time*, and follow-up time is what longitudinal designs (cohort and trial) collect. Cross-sectional designs do not; they have no denominator for "events per unit of time" because they never observed time.

**Person-time** is the sum of the time each patient was at risk in the study. A patient followed for 3 years contributes 3 person-years; a patient who had the outcome at year 1 contributes 1 person-year (and is no longer at risk after the event); a patient who dropped out at month 6 contributes 0.5 person-years. The denominator for the incidence rate is the total person-time, which is why incidence rate honors unequal follow-up while cumulative incidence does not.

**Prospective vs retrospective cohort.** The difference is when the data were collected, not the analytic structure. A prospective cohort collects baseline data, defines the cohort, then waits. A retrospective cohort defines the cohort from historical data and reconstructs follow-up from records that already exist. Both produce the same kind of inference (incidence, rate, HR, RR over a time window). The retrospective version is constrained by what was recorded; the prospective version is constrained by what you can afford to wait for.

The Galloway 2011 BSRBR paper cited in Track 1's go-deeper is a real-world example of a prospective cohort that gave rates per 1000 person-years and an HR. The same question asked in a cross-sectional design ("among current TNFi users, what fraction has had a serious infection in the past year?") would have given a prevalence-like number that could not have been turned into an HR.

## 4. Survival analysis: the intuition

Survival analysis is the right way to handle time-to-event outcomes when patients have unequal follow-up. The conceptual moves are simpler than the math.

**The Kaplan-Meier estimator** answers one question: at each time point T, what fraction of the original cohort is still event-free? The curve starts at 1.0 (everyone event-free at time 0) and steps down each time an event occurs. The step size is proportional to the fraction of currently-at-risk patients who had the event at that time. A patient who is *censored* (left the study without having the event) is removed from the at-risk pool but does not cause a step down.

**Censoring is the trickiest idea.** A censored patient is not the same as a patient who did not have the event. A censored patient is a patient *we do not know about* past the censoring time. They may have had the event the next day. The KM estimator handles this by assuming that censoring is independent of the event of interest (a censored patient has the same future risk as a non-censored patient still at risk at that time). When that assumption is wrong (for example, sicker patients drop out and we treat their dropouts as censoring), the estimator is biased.

**Tick marks on a KM curve indicate censored patients.** If a curve drops out of view at time T with several tick marks just before, the trailing portion of the curve is based on a small at-risk pool and should be read with caution.

**The log-rank test** compares whole curves. It asks: at each event time, are events distributed across groups in proportion to the size of each group's at-risk pool? Summed across all event times, do the groups behave the same? The test produces a chi-square statistic and a p-value. It is the standard unadjusted test for whether two survival curves differ.

**The hazard ratio (HR) summarizes the difference into one number.** Cox proportional-hazards regression gives an HR per unit of exposure with optional covariate adjustment. The HR's plain-English reading: "events are happening X times as fast in group A as in group B, at every moment of follow-up." That reading depends on the **proportional-hazards assumption**: the ratio of hazards is constant over time. When the assumption holds (the curves diverge in a roughly parallel way), the HR is a useful summary. When it fails (the curves cross, fan out, or converge), the HR averages over time in a way that obscures the real story.

**Curves cross is the classic non-proportional-hazards pattern.** It can mean the treatment helps early and harms late (or vice versa), or that the two groups have a time-varying difference. When you see curves cross, the HR is not a useful summary; report KM curves directly and consider time-stratified analysis. The Galloway 2011 BSRBR observation that TNFi infection risk concentrates in the first six months is a real clinical example: the early-period hazards differ much more than the later-period hazards, which strains the constant-HR assumption.

## 5. The reflex this track is trying to install

When you read a paper:

1. **Identify the design** before reading the result. Five questions get you there.
2. **Match the claim to the design.** A cross-sectional study cannot make causal claims. A case-control study gives odds ratios, not risks. An observational cohort can show association but cannot rule out confounding.
3. **For survival figures, check the censoring** before reading the HR. Look at the tail of the curve and the number-at-risk table beneath it (if present).
4. **For an HR, check the curves.** If they cross, the HR is hiding the actual story.

A paper that names its design, reports its inclusion and exclusion criteria, and shows a KM with a number-at-risk table is doing the work of letting you read it carefully. A paper that buries any of those is asking you to trust the headline.
